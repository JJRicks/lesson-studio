6V6the beam power tetrode

A glowing wire, two grids, a pair of beam plates and an almost perfect vacuum. Put a weak AC sine wave on the control grid and the 6V6 hands back a copy about 19 times bigger, strong enough to drive a speaker. Guitar amps, jukeboxes and radios have relied on it since the late 1930s.

The 6V6 in one minute

The big idea

The 6V6 is a voltage-controlled valve for electrons. A small voltage on its control grid sets a large current from cathode to plate. Run that current through a load and a small signal becomes a large one.

    The amplifier

    A weak AC sine wave in, a big one out

    The big idea

    Bias the grid at −12.5 V so the tube idles half open. Add a weak sine wave on top: each volt of grid swing changes the plate current by about 4 mA, and the output transformer turns that current swing into a voltage swing about 19 times bigger at the plate.

    Follow the signal

    Step by step through the circuit

    1. Input. The weak AC sine passes through the coupling capacitor C. A capacitor blocks steady DC but lets a wiggle through, so the source can't upset the grid's bias.
    2. Bias. The 100 kΩ resistor ties the grid to −12.5 V. The sine rides on top: with a 1 V input, the grid swings between −13.5 V and −11.5 V. It never goes positive, so the grid draws almost no current.
    3. Transconductance. Each volt of grid swing changes the plate current by about 4.1 mA. That ratio is the tube's transconductance, gm = ΔIP / ΔVG ≈ 4.1 mA/V.
    4. Load. The plate current flows through the transformer's primary, which looks like a 5 kΩ load to the tube. Combined with the tube's own 50 kΩ plate resistance that's about 4.6 kΩ, and 4.1 mA × 4.6 kΩ ≈ 19 V of plate swing per volt of input.
    5. Upside down. Grid up → more current → bigger drop across the primary → plate voltage down. Compare ① and ② on the scope: the output is inverted. Your ear can't tell.
    6. Speaker. The speaker is only 8 Ω, but the tube wants about 5 kΩ. The transformer (about 25 turns to 1) trades the plate's high voltage and small current for low voltage and big current, like a gearbox trades speed for torque.
    Analogy · a hand on the tap

    The B+ supply is a water main under high pressure. The grid is a tap handle. Your weak sine wave is a hand wiggling the handle a few degrees back and forth. The flow through the pipe (plate current) wiggles in step, with far more power than your hand put in. The energy comes from the water main; the hand only controls it.

    Why does the plate swing above the 250 V supply?

    The transformer's primary is a big inductor. When the plate current drops, the inductor's magnetic field collapses and pushes the plate voltage above B+, giving back energy it stored on the other half-cycle. At full drive the plate swings from about 75 V up to about 460 V around its 250 V resting point.

    See it on the curves

    The load line

    Each grey curve shows how plate current depends on plate voltage for one fixed grid voltage. The blue load line is every combination of voltage and current the 5 kΩ load allows. The tube has to sit where both agree, so as the grid swings, the operating point (orange dot) slides up and down the line.

    6V6 plate curves · screen at 250 V

    Notice the curves go flat once the plate is above about 50 V: the plate current hardly cares about the plate voltage. That's the screen grid at work. The red dashed curve is the 12 W limit on plate heat.

    Turn it up

    Push it: clipping and harmonics

    Raise the input past about 12.5 V peak and the tube runs out of room:

    • Top of the grid swing: the grid reaches 0 V and starts collecting electrons itself (grid current). It can't go any higher, so that half of the wave flattens.
    • Bottom of the grid swing: the grid gets so negative the current stops altogether (cutoff, near −26 V), and the other half flattens too.

    The two halves flatten differently. That lopsided clipping adds harmonics: extra tones at 2, 3, 4… times the input frequency. A 2nd harmonic is an octave up, which blends in smoothly; that's a big part of the "warm" sound people like in single-ended tube amps. The datasheet allows about 8% distortion at the full 4.5 W.

    Harmonics in the output · % of the fundamental

    Try 1 V (almost pure), 12.5 V (full rated power) and 25 V (heavily overdriven), then hit Listen in the amplifier panel above.

    Bias and red-plating. Class A means current flows through the whole cycle, so the 6V6 idles at 45 mA × 250 V ≈ 11 W of heat even in silence. Slide the bias toward 0 V in the amplifier panel and the idle heat passes the 12 W rating: the plate starts to glow red ("red-plating") and the tube dies young. Slide it toward −20 V and it runs cool, but clips early on one side.

    Going deeper

    Why "beam" tetrode?

    The big idea

    Electrons hit the plate hard enough to knock other electrons loose. In a plain tetrode, when the plate dips below the screen voltage, the screen steals those loose electrons and the plate current collapses. The 6V6's dense beam pushes them back home.

    An electron arriving at 250 V is moving at about 9,000 km/s (3% of the speed of light). When it slams into the plate it can knock out one or more secondary electrons. Where they go next depends on which nearby electrode is more positive.

    Grid fixed at −12.5 V, screen at 250 V (green dotted line). Cyan dots are secondary electrons. Set the plate to 150 V and switch between the two tube types.

    • Plain tetrode: with the plate below the screen, the screen is the most positive thing around, so it collects the secondaries. Between about 50 and 230 V the plate current sags, even goes negative. That dip is the tetrode kink: there, more plate voltage gives less current, which makes plain tetrodes poor power-output tubes.
    • Beam tetrode: beam-forming plates (tied to the cathode) squeeze the electrons into dense sheets. All that negative charge between screen and plate forms a low-voltage pocket (a "virtual cathode") that turns the secondaries around and sends them back to the plate. No kink.
    • Bonus: the screen-grid wires sit in the shadow of the control-grid wires, so fewer electrons hit the screen. Less wasted current, more efficiency.
    Analogy · splashback

    Throw mud at a wall and bits splash back. In a plain tetrode, a fan behind you (the screen) sucks the splashes away whenever the wall is "lower" than the fan. The beam tetrode packs a crowd between you and the wall that shoves the splashes right back onto the wall.

    A bit of history

    The pentode solves the same problem with a third grid (the suppressor), and Philips held the patent on it. Engineers at EMI in Britain found a way around it: shape the beam so the electrons themselves do the suppressing. They patented the beam tetrode in 1933. RCA's 6L6 made the idea famous in 1936, and the smaller 6V6 soon followed.
