Foundations

The big idea

Everything in this homework rests on four ideas: what current and voltage are, how doped silicon makes a one-way valve (a diode), how two of those valves back to back make a transistor, and how to read the transistor's symbol.

Foundation 1

Current, voltage, and the units on your plots

Current (I)

How much electric charge flows past a point each second. Measured in amperes (A).

Like water: liters per second through a pipe.

Voltage (V)

The electrical "push" between two points. Measured in volts (V). Always a difference between two places.

Like water pressure difference between two ends of a pipe.

Transistor currents are small, so the plots use metric prefixes. Read them like this:

1 Aamperea toaster
1 mAmilliamp = 0.001 Aan LED
1 µAmicroamp = 0.000001 Aa watch

So 1 mA = 1000 µA. A base current of 106 µA is the same as 0.106 mA or 0.000106 A.

LTspice spelling. LTspice writes µ as u (so 100u = 100 µA) and milli as m (so 10m = 0.01). Capital M also means milli in SPICE, not mega. Mega is meg.

How to read the subscripts

  • VBE means "voltage at the Base minus voltage at the Emitter". The order of letters matters: VBE = VB − VE.
  • VCE = VC − VE. VEC = VE − VC (used for pnp, so the number comes out positive).
  • IC, IB, IE are the currents in the collector, base, and emitter wires.
Conventional current vs electrons

Engineers draw current flowing from + to −. This is called conventional current. The electrons actually drift the opposite way (they are negative). Every arrow, equation, and SPICE number in this homework uses conventional current. You only need electrons when picturing what happens inside the silicon.

Foundation 2

From plain silicon to a one-way valve

Pure silicon barely conducts. Engineers "dope" it with a tiny amount of another element to give it charges that can move:

n-type

Add phosphorus. Each atom brings one extra free electron. The moving charges are negative, hence "n".

p-type

Add boron. Each atom leaves a spot missing an electron, called a hole. Holes move and act like positive charges, hence "p".

Analogy · holes

Picture a row of theater seats with one empty seat. When each person slides over one seat, the empty seat travels down the row. A hole is that empty seat: really it's electrons shuffling, but it's easier to track the gap.

Put p-type next to n-type and you get a pn junction, which is a diode (you met these in the diode homework):

  • Push current from p to n (forward bias) and almost nothing happens until about 0.6–0.7 V. Then current rises exponentially.
  • Push the other way (reverse bias) and essentially no current flows.

Keep this in your pocket: a forward-biased junction's current grows exponentially with its voltage. Part A is this fact, measured on a transistor.

Foundation 3

Two junctions make a transistor

An npn BJT is a sandwich: n-type, a very thin slice of p-type, then n-type again. That gives it three terminals and two junctions.

LayerNameJob
n+ (heavily doped)EmitterEmits electrons into the base.
p (very thin)BaseThe control. A small current here sets the big current.
nCollectorCollects the electrons that make it through the base.

What happens inside, step by step

  1. Turn on the base–emitter junction by making VBE about 0.7 V. Like any diode, it now lets electrons pour from the emitter into the base.
  2. The base is very thin, so most electrons zip straight across before they can find a hole to fill.
  3. The collector sits at a higher voltage (VCE = 5 V here), so it pulls those electrons in. That stream is the collector current IC.
  4. A few electrons get stuck in the base and recombine with holes. Replacing them takes a small base current IB.
  5. The fraction that gets stuck is roughly fixed. So IC is a roughly fixed multiple of IB. That multiple is the current gain β ("beta").
Analogy · the crowded lobby

A crowd runs from an entrance (emitter) through a narrow lobby (base) to an exit door (collector). The lobby has a coffee stand. About 1 person in 290 stops for coffee (base current). The rest go out the door (collector current). If you see 1 extra person at the coffee stand, you know about 290 extra people went through the door.

Charge can't vanish, so whatever flows in must flow out (Kirchhoff's current law):

IE = IC + IBExample from Part A: 31.04 mA + 0.107 mA = 31.15 mA leaves through the emitter.
Go deeper: where do "2N3904" and "2N3906" come from?

In the old JEDEC numbering, 1N parts have one junction (diodes, like the 1N4148) and 2N parts have two junctions (transistors). The 2N3904 is a popular small-signal npn. The 2N3906 is its pnp twin, built to mirror it.

The base got its name historically: the first transistors were built on top of a slab of germanium that served as the mechanical base of the device.

Foundation 4

Reading the transistor symbol

npn: emitter arrow points out. Mnemonic: "Not Pointing iN".

pnp: emitter arrow points in. Mnemonic: "Points iN Proudly".

  • The arrow is always on the emitter and shows the direction conventional current flows through it.
  • The flat bar is the base. The other diagonal is the collector.
  • In LTspice the part is labelled Q1. A trace named Ic(Q1) means "current flowing into Q1's collector". Ib(Q1) is the same for the base.
Quick check: if IB = 50 µA and β = 200, what is IC?

IC = β × IB = 200 × 50 µA = 10,000 µA = 10 mA.

Part A · Problems 1–3 · 7 points · 2N3904

npn: IC and IB versus VBE

The big idea

Hold the collector at 5 V and slowly raise VBE from 0.6 V to 0.8 V. Both currents rise exponentially, and IC stays roughly 290 times IB the whole way.

Topic A1

The common-emitter setup

The circuit has two loops, and the emitter wire belongs to both. That shared ("common") emitter gives the setup its name.

Homework schematic · Part A
Base (input) loop: VBE → base → emitter → groundCollector (output) loop: VCE → collector → emitter → ground
  • VBE source: the control knob. LTspice will sweep it from 0.6 V to 0.8 V.
  • VCE source (5 V): the supply that pulls electrons into the collector. It stays fixed.
  • Ground (the triangles): the 0 V reference. All three triangles are the same node.
Why hold VCE fixed?

Good experiments change one thing at a time. To learn how a car's speed depends on the gas pedal, you test on a flat road. Here VBE is the pedal and VCE is the road. Part B does the opposite: it fixes the base and changes VCE.

Topic A2

The exponential law

The base–emitter junction is a diode, so the collector current follows the diode (Shockley) equation:

IC ≈ IS · eVBE / VT IS ≈ 1×10−14 A is a tiny constant set by the device (the 2N3904 model uses exactly this). VT = kT/q ≈ 25.9 mV at room temperature is the "thermal voltage".

What "exponential" means: adding a fixed amount to the input multiplies the output by a fixed factor. For a BJT, every extra ≈ 60 mV of VBE multiplies IC by 10.

The 60 mV rule
×10.1
bar is on a log scale: each quarter = ×10

Multiplier = eΔV/25.9 mV. 59.6 mV gives exactly ×10, because 25.9 mV × ln(10) = 59.6 mV.

Analogy · folding paper

Fold a sheet of paper and its thickness doubles. Fold again: doubles again. Each fold adds the same effort but multiplies the result. Each +60 mV is one "fold" that multiplies current by 10. That's why the graph looks flat, then shoots up.

The base current follows the same curve, shrunk by β: IB = IC / β. Both panes in your LTspice plot will have the same shape with different y-axis scales (mA on top, µA below).

Simulated 2N3904 · .dc VBE 0.6 0.8

Drag on the plot (or focus it and use ← →, hold Shift for 10 mV jumps) to move the cursor. Switch to Log: an exponential becomes a straight line, rising one decade every ~60 mV.

Go deeper: why the top of the curve bends slightly below a pure exponential

At small currents the model matches the formula exactly: going from 600 mV to 650 mV multiplies IC by 6.86, and e50/25.9 = 6.9.

At large currents two things steal some of the push: the transistor's internal resistances (20 Ω in the base, 0.1 Ω in the emitter) drop part of VBE, and the base gets flooded with so many electrons that it behaves differently ("high-level injection", set by the model's IKF = 0.4 A). You can see this as β drifting downward in the cursor readout as you move right.

Topic A3

Reading the DC sweep command

A DC sweep tells LTspice to solve the circuit many times, nudging one source a little each time, and connect the results into a graph. It is a set of snapshots, not a movie: time does not pass. Click each piece of the command:

Click a token above.

From 0.6 to 0.8 in steps of 0.01 gives (0.8 − 0.6) / 0.01 + 1 = 21 points. LTspice draws straight lines between them. 750 mV is exactly one of the points, which is why the homework picks it.

"Linear" means equal step sizes. LTspice also offers Octave and Decade sweeps (steps that multiply), handy for frequencies, not needed here.

Topic A4

Current gain, βdc

βdc = ICIBAlso written hFE on datasheets. No units: amps divided by amps.
  • "dc" means you use the actual (total, steady) current values at one operating point.
  • It answers: "How many milliamps of collector current do I get per milliamp of base current, right here?"
  • Units trap: IC is in mA and IB is in µA. Convert both to the same unit before dividing, or you'll be off by 1000.
Analogy · power steering

A light touch on the wheel (base current) steers a heavy car (collector current). β is the assist ratio. The transistor does not create energy: the big current comes from the VCE supply. The base just decides how much of it flows.

β is only roughly constant. Move the cursor in the plot above: it drifts from about 313 at 600 mV to about 249 at 800 mV. Datasheets list a wide range (100 to 300 for the 2N3904 at 10 mA) because real parts vary. The SPICE model is one "typical" part.

Refresher: significant figures

Significant figures are the digits that carry real information, counted from the first non-zero digit. "At least 3 significant figures" means write 291 rather than 290, or 0.0310 rather than 0.03.

Don't round the cursor values before dividing. Round only the final answer.

Part B · Problems 4–6 · 7 points · 2N3904

npn: the family of IC versus VCE curves

The big idea

Feed the base a fixed current, sweep the collector voltage from 0 to 10 V, and repeat for four base currents. Each curve goes flat almost immediately: past about 0.5 V, the collector current stops caring about VCE and only cares about IB.

Topic B1

Driving the base with a current source

The VBE voltage source is replaced by a current source named IB. Now we set the base current directly and ask how IC responds to VCE.

Homework schematic · Part B
  • A current source forces a set current through itself, using whatever voltage it takes. The arrow shows which way it pushes. Here it pushes current into the base.
  • Starting value 100µ = 100 µA. The sweep will step it to 200, 300, 400 µA.
  • VCE shows "0." because its value is set by the sweep.
Analogy · metering pump

A voltage source is like a water tower: fixed pressure, flow depends on the pipes. A current source is like a metering pump: fixed flow, it works as hard as needed to keep it.

Topic B2

Nested sweeps: one command, four curves

Click a token above. Solid tokens are the inner sweep, dashed tokens the outer sweep.

The first source listed is the inner (fast) loop. The second is the outer (slow) loop. It works like nested loops in code:

for IB in 100µ, 200µ, 300µ, 400µ:      # outer loop: 4 values
    for VCE from 0 to 10 V, step 10 mV: # inner loop: 1,001 values
        solve the circuit, record Ic(Q1)

That's 4 × 1,001 = 4,004 solves, drawn as 4 curves, one per base current. In LTspice these are called "steps", and the colors run green, blue, red, cyan for 100, 200, 300, 400 µA.

Analogy · clock hands

The minute hand (VCE) sweeps a full lap for every single tick of the hour hand (IB). Press Run sweep below and watch the counter.

Topic B3

Reading the family of curves

Simulated 2N3904 · .dc VCE 0 10 10m IB 100u 400u 100u

Drag on the plot to move the VCE cursor. The table shows every curve at the cursor; the two highlighted rows are your cursors.

RegionWhereWhat's happening
SaturationVCE below ~0.2–0.3 V (the steep rise on the left)The collector isn't high enough to pull electrons hard. Both junctions are forward-biased. IC depends strongly on VCE. Used for "fully on" switches.
ActiveVCE ≳ 0.5 V (the flat part)Base–emitter on, base–collector reverse-biased. The collector grabs nearly every electron, so IC ≈ β·IB no matter what VCE is. Amplifiers live here.
CutoffIB = 0 (not plotted)Base–emitter off. IC ≈ 0. The "off" switch state.

Two more things to notice:

  • A slight upward tilt. IC rises about 1% per volt in the flat region. This is the Early effect: higher VCE makes the effective base a little thinner, so fewer electrons get lost in it.
  • Curves bunch up. At 5 V the gaps between curves are about 26, 23.6, then 21.8 mA. Equal steps in IB give shrinking steps in IC, because β droops at high current. This matters in Topic B5.
Go deeper: the Early voltage

If you extend the flat parts of all the curves backward to the left, they meet near VCE ≈ −VA, the Early voltage. The 2N3904 model uses VA = 100 V. That gives IC ≈ βIB(1 + VCE/VA), about 1% more current per volt.

Topic B4

Independent vs dependent sources

To answer Problem 6 you need two ideas from circuit theory. First, what does each kind of source look like on a current-vs-voltage graph?

Current source

Flat (horizontal) line. Current stays the same whatever the voltage.

Voltage source

Vertical line. Voltage stays the same whatever the current.

Second, who sets the source's value?

Independent source

Its value is fixed by itself. A 9 V battery is 9 V no matter what else is in the circuit.

Dependent (controlled) source

Its value is set by some other current or voltage in the circuit. Drawn as a diamond.

Look at the family of curves for VCE ≥ 0.5 V: each curve is flat (a current source), and which flat line you're on is decided by IB (dependent). So in the active region the BJT acts as a current-controlled current source (CCCS):

IC = β · IBThe output current is a copy of the input current, scaled up by β.
What the collector "looks like" to the circuit
Analogy · cruise control on a hill

Cruise control holds your speed (the current) steady whether the road is flat or steep (the voltage). That's a current source. The speed it holds is whatever the driver sets with the dial (IB). That's dependent.

Topic B5

βac: gain for the change

βac = ΔICΔIBΔ ("delta") means "change in". Measure both IC values at the same VCE, so IB is the only thing that changed.

βdc = IC/IB

Total ÷ total. "How much collector current per unit of base current, overall?"

βac = ΔIC/ΔIB

Change ÷ change. "If I add a little more base current, how much more collector current do I get?" This is the gain a small audio signal riding on the base actually sees.

Analogy · average vs marginal tax rate

Your average tax rate is total tax ÷ total income (like βdc). Your marginal rate is the tax on your next dollar (like βac). They differ when the rate changes with income. β changes with current, so βac ≠ βdc.

At VCE = 5 V, βdc is about 292 on the 100 µA curve and 276 on the 200 µA curve. Because β is falling as current grows, each extra microamp of base current buys less than the average. So βac comes out lower than both: you'll see it in Problem 5.

Part C · Problems 7–8 · 6 points · 2N3906

pnp: the family of IC versus VEC curves

The big idea

A pnp transistor is an npn in a mirror: the layers swap, every voltage and current flips direction, and holes do the carrying. The curves have the same shape, but LTspice draws them upside-down because it counts current leaving a terminal as negative.

Topic C1

The mirror image

Homework schematic · Part C
Notice the emitter is now on top, connected to VEC's + terminal. The IB arrow points away from the base.

Why is the pnp's gain lower?

Holes move about 2 to 3 times slower through silicon than electrons. Slower carriers spend longer crossing the base, so more of them get lost along the way. More lost carriers means more base current for the same collector current, so β is lower. The SPICE models reflect this: BF = 200 for the 2N3906 vs 300 for the 2N3904.

Topic C2

SPICE's sign rule

SPICE reports a terminal current as positive when it flows into the terminal, and negative when it flows out.

  • In a pnp, collector current flows out of the collector, so Ic(Q1) is negative. The curves hang below zero.
  • The IB source pulls 100 µA out of the base, so Ib(Q1) reads −100 µA even though the source is labelled 100µ.
  • Fix: work with magnitudes (absolute values, written |x|). The curve "closest to zero" has the smallest |IB|.
Analogy · a bank statement

Deposits show as +$50, withdrawals as −$50. Either way, $50 moved. The sign tells direction; the magnitude tells amount. For β we care about amount.

βac = |ΔIC||ΔIB|

Same shape as the npn family, flipped below zero and about two-thirds as tall.

Common mistakes

  • Dividing mA by µA without converting (answers off by 1000).
  • Reading the two cursors at different VCE values. Both must say Horz: 5V.
  • Putting the cursor on the wrong curve. Lowest |IC| = smallest |IB| = 100 µA.

  • Panicking at negative pnp currents. Take magnitudes.
  • Mixing up βdc (IC/IB) with βac (ΔIC/ΔIB).
