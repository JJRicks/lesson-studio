Foundations

The big idea

You only need two circuit laws (Ohm's law and Kirchhoff's voltage law) and a three-rule model of the transistor. Then one habit: guess the transistor's region, solve, and check that the guess holds.

Foundation 1

Ohm's law and Kirchhoff's voltage law

Ohm's law

V = I · R    ⇔    I = V / R

The voltage across a resistor equals the current through it times its resistance. Like water: a narrower pipe (bigger R) needs more pressure (V) for the same flow (I).

Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL)

Walk around any closed loop and add up the voltages. The rises exactly equal the drops. Like hiking a loop trail: when you get back to the car, you're at the same altitude you started.

Two shortcuts that make this homework fast:

Voltage acrossResistorCurrent
1 V1 kΩ1 mA
1 V100 kΩ10 µA
1 mA × 1 kΩ= 1 V

So volts ÷ kΩ gives mA, and mA × kΩ gives volts. No powers of ten needed.

  • Ground (the triangle symbol) is the 0 V reference. Every voltage in the problems, like Vout, is measured from ground.
  • Prefixes: 1 mA = 0.001 A, 1 µA = 0.000001 A, so 1 mA = 1000 µA. 1 kΩ = 1000 Ω.
Foundation 2

The 3-rule BJT model

A real transistor is complicated. For hand calculations, three rules capture almost everything (these are the facts you measured in last week's LTspice homework):

Rule 1 · the base is a diode

When the transistor is on, the base–emitter junction drops a fixed 0.7 V. npn: VBE = 0.7 V (base above emitter). pnp: VEB = 0.7 V (base below emitter).

Rule 2 · the collector copies the base

In the active region, IC = β · IB. β (beta) is the current gain, given in each problem.

Rule 3 · nothing gets lost

All current in must come out: IE = IC + IB.

Analogy · a remote-controlled valve

The base is a small control pipe with a fixed "toll" of 0.7 V to enter. Whatever trickle you send through it opens the big collector valve by β times as much. The big flow comes from the VCC supply; the base only decides how much.

Why exactly 0.7 V?

The base–emitter junction's current is exponential in voltage: each extra 60 mV multiplies current by 10. Over the currents in this homework (tens of µA), VBE only changes by tens of millivolts, so treating it as a constant 0.7 V is accurate enough and makes the algebra easy.

Foundation 3

The three regions of operation

Rule 2 only holds in one of three regions. Half the points in this homework ask which region the transistor is in.

RegionHow to spot itWhat's trueUsed as
CutoffBase drive below 0.7 V, so IB = 0IC = 0. No drop across R2.Switch off
ActiveVCE (pnp: VEC) ≥ 0.2 VIC = β · IB exactly as the model says.Amplifier
Saturationβ · IB would need VCE < 0.2 VVCE stuck at ≈ 0.2 V. IC < β · IB, limited by R2.Switch fully on

The 0.2 V figure is VCE(sat), the smallest voltage a saturated transistor drops. It's the usual textbook value and the one this lesson uses.

Analogy · faucet and drainpipe

The base sets how far the faucet is opened (β · IB is how much water it wants to let through). R2 is a drainpipe that can only carry so much. If the faucet asks for more than the pipe can carry, the flow is capped by the pipe, not the faucet. That capped state is saturation.

The load line makes this visual. R2 and VCC allow only certain combinations of IC and VCE: a straight line from "all voltage, no current" (VCE = VCC) to "all current, no voltage" (IC = VCC/R2). The transistor's operating point must sit on that line. In the playground at the top, push β up and watch the dot slide left until it hits the red saturation strip and stops.

Foundation 4

The method: assume, solve, check

You can't know the region before you calculate, so you guess, solve, and then check that the guess is consistent.

Step 1 · base loop

Is the base drive above 0.7 V? If not, it's cutoff: IB = IC = 0. If yes, find IB with KVL around the base loop.

Step 2 · assume active

IC = β · IB.

Step 3 · collector loop

Use Ohm's law on R2 to find Vout, then VCE (npn) or VEC (pnp).

Step 4 · check
VCE ≥ 0.2 V
Guess holds. Active. Your numbers are final.
VCE < 0.2 V (or negative)
Guess failed. Saturation. Set VCE = 0.2 V and redo IC and Vout from that.
Analogy · guess and check

It's like guessing the answer to "I'm thinking of a number; double it and add 3 to get 11." Guess 4, check: 2·4 + 3 = 11. Correct. If the check fails, the guess was wrong and you try the other option. Here there are only two options for an on transistor, so the second guess is always right.

Part A · Problems 1–4 · 9 points

npn circuits

The big idea

V1 pushes a small current through R1 into the base. β multiplies it into a collector current that flows down from VCC through R2. The bigger that current, the more voltage R2 eats, and the lower Vout sits.

Topic A1 · for Problem 1

Finding the base current

Follow the base loop: start at ground, up through V1, across R1, into the base, out the emitter, back to ground. KVL says the rise from V1 is used up by two drops:

V1 = IBR1 + VBE⇒IB = V1 − 0.7 VR1Whatever voltage is left after the 0.7 V "toll" sits across R1. Ohm's law turns it into current.
Where V1 goes (R1 = 100 kΩ)

Blue: voltage across R1, sets IB. Amber: the fixed 0.7 V across the base–emitter junction.

  • Notice the collector side (VCC, R2) doesn't appear. With the emitter tied to ground, the base loop doesn't care what the collector is doing.
  • If V1 < 0.7 V, the junction never turns on: IB = 0 and the transistor is in cutoff.

Topic A2 · for Problem 2

Finding the collector current

Once you have IB, Rule 2 gives the collector current directly, assuming the active region:

IC = β · IBβ is unitless, so IC comes out in the same unit as IB. Then convert: 1000 µA = 1 mA.
  • β is given in the problem. Real transistors vary a lot (a 2N3904 can be anywhere from 100 to 300), which is why each problem states it.
  • Strictly, you should confirm the active-region assumption with the check in Step 4 of the method. It takes one line, and Problem 2's reveal shows it.
Analogy · exchange rate

β is an exchange rate: every base microamp "buys" β collector microamps. With β = 100, 20 µA of base current buys 2000 µA = 2 mA of collector current.

Topic A3 · for Problems 3 and 4

Finding Vout and the region of operation

Vout is the collector voltage. Follow the collector loop from VCC down through R2: the collector current makes R2 drop ICR2, and whatever is left is Vout.

Vout = VC = VCC − ICR2The emitter is at ground (0 V), so VCE = VC − 0 = Vout. Here Vout is VCE.

More collector current → bigger drop across R2 → lower Vout. When β·IB is so big that Vout would fall below 0.2 V, the transistor saturates instead.

Quick way to check saturation

The most current R2 can ever pass (with VCE at its 0.2 V floor) is:

IC(max) = VCC − 0.2 VR2If β·IB is below this, the transistor is active. If β·IB is above it, it's saturated and IC = IC(max).
If you find…RegionVout (npn)
V1 ≤ 0.7 VCutoffVCC (no current, no drop)
VCC − βIBR2 ≥ 0.2 VActiveVCC − βIBR2
VCC − βIBR2 < 0.2 VSaturation≈ 0.2 V
Watch the edge. Problems in this homework are tuned to land close to the 0.2 V line. Carry at least 4 digits through the calculation so you don't round your way into the wrong region.

Part B · Problems 5–8 · 9 points

pnp circuits

The big idea

A pnp is an npn flipped upside down. The emitter connects to VCC, current flows out of the base and collector toward ground, and Vout is the voltage the collector current builds up across R2. Same four steps, with signs.

Topic B1 · for Problem 5

pnp base current and the sign convention

In these circuits the emitter sits at VCC. For the pnp to turn on, the base must be 0.7 V below the emitter (VEB = 0.7 V). Current flows into the emitter, out of the base, and down through R1 to ground.

Base loop: VCC → emitter → base (0.7 V drop) → R1 → ground.

VCC = VEB + |IB|R1⇒|IB| = VCC − 0.7 VR1
Where VCC goes (R1 = 100 kΩ)

Amber: 0.7 V from emitter down to base. Blue: the rest, across R1, which sets |IB|.

The sign rule

Current flowing into a transistor terminal is positive. Current flowing out of a terminal is negative. (This is the same convention SPICE uses, which you saw last week.)

Example (not from the homework)

|IB| = 20 µA and β = 100.

  • IB flows out: −20 µA
  • IC flows out: −2.00 mA
  • IE flows in: +2.02 mA

Check: with signs, the three always add to zero: 2.02 − 2.00 − 0.02 = 0. Charge in = charge out.

Analogy · bank statement

Deposits are +, withdrawals are −. Taking $43 out of an account shows as −$43. The base current of a pnp is a withdrawal from the transistor, so it gets a minus sign. The size of the current doesn't change.

Topic B2 · for Problem 6

pnp collector current

Rule 2 still holds, and the sign carries through: base current flows out, so collector current flows out too.

IC = β · IBWith IB negative, IC comes out negative automatically. Or work with magnitudes, |IC| = β|IB|, and add the minus sign at the end.
  • The collector current flows out of the collector and down through R2 to ground.
  • The emitter carries the sum: |IE| = |IC| + |IB|, flowing in (positive).

Topic B3 · for Problems 7 and 8

pnp Vout and the region of operation

In the pnp circuits R2 hangs below the collector, down to ground. The collector current flowing down through it lifts Vout above ground:

Vout = VC = |IC| · R2Then VEC = VE − VC = VCC − Vout.

This is the opposite of the npn: more collector current means a higher Vout. It keeps rising until Vout gets within 0.2 V of VCC. Then the pnp saturates.

If you find…RegionVout (pnp)
VCC ≤ 0.7 VCutoff0 V (no current)
VCC − β|IB|R2 ≥ 0.2 VActiveβ|IB|R2
VCC − β|IB|R2 < 0.2 VSaturation≈ VCC − 0.2 V
pnp circuit playground

Same layout as Problems 5–8. Raise R2 or β and watch Vout climb toward VCC until it pins 0.2 V below it.

What happens to the current in saturation?

In saturation the base current is still set by the base loop (VCC − 0.7)/R1, because nothing in that loop changed. But the collector current is now capped by R2: |IC| = (VCC − 0.2)/R2. The ratio |IC|/|IB| drops below β. Engineers call that ratio the "forced β".

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting to subtract the 0.7 V before dividing by R1.
  • Multiplying µA by β and reporting the result as mA without converting.
  • Rounding IC early on an edge case (Problem 3) and landing in the wrong region.
  • Reporting the active-assumption Vout after the check failed (Problem 8).
  • Dropping the minus sign on pnp IB and IC.
  • Using the npn formula VCC − ICR2 on a pnp circuit, where R2 is below the collector.
