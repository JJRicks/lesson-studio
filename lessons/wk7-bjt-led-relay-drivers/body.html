BJT LED & Relay Drivers

A microcontroller pin is a tiny switch that can only push a little current at a low voltage. Put a transistor after it and that pin can light an LED, or click a relay that runs a 120 V lamp. This page builds every idea you need from scratch, then works all seven problems step by step, with each final answer hidden until you click.

Start here · no prior knowledge needed

Why a pin can't do the job alone

A microcontroller, like an Arduino or an ESP32, talks to the world through digital output pins. Each pin has two states:

  • HIGH: the pin is connected inside the chip to its supply, usually 3.3 V or 5 V.
  • LOW: the pin is connected to ground, 0 V.

Two limits make a bare pin a poor driver:

  • Current. A pin is rated for a few milliamps, often 4 to 20 mA at most. Pull more and its voltage sags, it heats up, and eventually it fails. A bright LED wants 20 mA. A relay coil can want more.
  • Voltage. A pin can never give more than its own supply. A 12 V relay won't move on 3.3 V.

The fix is a transistor used as a switch. The pin supplies a small control current. The transistor then lets a much bigger current flow from a separate, stronger supply through the load. The pin only ever sees the small current.

Analogy · the doorbell button

A doorbell button can't power the bell, but it can tell something else to. Your finger (the pin) does very little work, and the house wiring (the 5 V or 12 V supply) does the heavy lifting. The transistor is the part that listens to your finger and connects the house wiring.

The pin decides when. The supply provides how much. The transistor connects the two.

Part A · Problems 1–2 · 16 pts

The npn LED driver

The big idea

A 3.3 V pin pushes a small base current into an npn transistor through R1, which switches it fully on. Then 5 V drives 20 mA through R2 and the white LED, with R2 setting that current.

Topic 1 · from first principles

The BJT as a switch

A bipolar junction transistor (BJT) has three legs:

  • Base (B): the control input. The pin connects here, through a resistor.
  • Collector (C): where the load's current comes in, for an npn.
  • Emitter (E): where everything leaves, to ground for an npn. The arrow on the symbol sits on the emitter and points the way current flows.

The base-to-emitter path behaves like a diode. Below about 0.6 V almost nothing flows. Once current does flow, the voltage across it, \(V_{BE}\), stays close to 0.7 V. That's the "usual 0.7 V approximation" the homework asks for.

Here's the useful part. A small base current \(I_B\) lets a much larger collector current \(I_C\) flow. When nothing else limits it, the ratio is the transistor's current gain, β (also written hFE):

\[I_C = \beta\, I_B \qquad I_E = I_B + I_C\]

A transistor in a circuit sits in one of three regions:

RegionWhat the base getsWhat you seeUsed as
cutoffNo base current (pin LOW)\(I_C \approx 0\). The transistor is an open switch.switch OFF
activeSome base current\(I_C = \beta I_B\), with volts left over across C–E.amplifier
saturationMore than enough base current\(V_{CE}\) drops to almost 0. The load sets \(I_C\), and \(I_C \lt \beta I_B\).switch ON
Analogy · a valve on a water main

The base current is your hand on a valve, and the collector current is the water. A small turn lets a lot of water through (active). Crank it all the way open and the valve stops mattering, because the pipe after it decides how much water flows (saturation). Let go and it shuts (cutoff).

A switch lives at the two ends: cutoff for OFF, saturation for ON. Never in the middle.

Quick check: in which region does an npn have \(V_{CE} \approx 0\)?

Saturation. The transistor is fully on, like a closed switch, so almost no voltage is left across it.

Topic 2 · the load

An LED needs a resistor to set its current

An LED (light-emitting diode) is a diode that gives off light when current flows forward through it, from its anode (the triangle's flat side) to its cathode (the bar). Two facts matter here:

  • Forward voltage \(V_F\). An LED only conducts once it has roughly its forward voltage across it. The colour sets it: red is about 2 V, green about 2–3 V, and white or blue about 3–3.4 V. The homework uses 3.2 V for white and 2.7 V for green.
  • Exponential current. Above \(V_F\), a tiny extra voltage makes the current shoot up. 0.2 V too much can mean ten times the current.

So you can't set an LED's current by picking its voltage. The fix is a series resistor. The supply's extra voltage lands on the resistor, and Ohm's law then sets the current:

\[I = \frac{V_S - V_F}{R}\]
Analogy · a ledge and a ramp

The LED is a ledge with a cliff: nothing happens until you reach the edge, then everything at once. The resistor is a gentle ramp before it, which controls how fast you arrive.

An LED and its resistor · live

The solid curve is the LED model from the homework. The dashed line is every current the resistor allows, \(I = (V_S - V)/R\). The circuit sits where they cross. Shrink the resistor and the dot climbs the steep part. Drag it to 0 Ω and the model says the LED would try to pull amps, so a real one burns out.

The resistor sets the current. The LED just takes about \(V_F\) off the top.

Topic 3 · the key idea of a switch

Saturation, and why we design with βmin = 10

Once the LED and R2 are chosen, they cap the current at roughly \((5 - 3.2)/R_2\), even with the transistor fully on. Call it what the load allows.

  • If \(\beta I_B\) is less than what the load allows, the transistor is the bottleneck. It sits in the active region, half on, with volts across C–E, and the LED is dim.
  • If \(\beta I_B\) is more than what the load allows, the transistor can't pass all it is "allowed" to. It turns fully on, \(V_{CE}\) collapses to tens of millivolts, and the load sets \(I_C\). That's saturation.

In saturation \(I_C/I_B\) comes out smaller than β, because \(I_C\) is capped while \(I_B\) is whatever you supply. That ratio is called the forced beta.

The rule of thumb

To make sure of saturation, choose the base current from a forced beta of 10, whatever the datasheet's β is:

\[I_B = \frac{I_{C}}{\beta_{min}} = \frac{I_C}{10}\]

Why 10, when a 2N3904 has a β of 100 to 300? Because β varies a lot from part to part and with temperature, and a switch must work with the weakest part on its worst day. Datasheets also quote their saturation voltage at \(I_C/I_B = 10\). For the 2N3904, \(V_{CE(sat)}\) is at most 0.2 V at 10 mA and 1 mA.

Saturation explorer · 3.3 V pin, 5 V supply, white LED

The solid curve is every \((V_{CE}, I_C)\) the transistor allows for your base current, from a Gummel-Poon model of the 2N3904. The faint curves are ¼, ½ and 2 times that current. The dashed curve is what the LED and R2 allow. It starts saturated. Drag β down to 60 and the same circuit drops out of saturation. Then shrink R1 until it's saturated again.

Give the base 1/10 of the collector current, and the switch works with any transistor.

Topic 4 · real parts

Standard ±5% resistor values (the E24 series)

You can't buy a 1234 Ω resistor. Manufacturers make a fixed menu of values. ±5% resistors come in the E24 series: 24 values per decade, each about 10% above the last.

1.01.11.21.31.51.61.82.02.22.42.73.03.33.63.94.34.75.15.66.26.87.58.29.1

Multiply any of them by 1, 10, 100, 1 k and so on. So 2.2 gives 2.2 Ω, 22 Ω, 220 Ω, 2.2 kΩ, 22 kΩ and up.

Why 24, spaced like that? A "5%" part can really be anywhere within ±5% of its label. Values about 10% apart mean the tolerance bands just about touch, so every resistance is covered with no waste.

How to round, as the homework does:

  1. Write your value as a number from 1 to 10 times a power of ten: 2100 Ω = 2.1 × 1 kΩ.
  2. Find the E24 values just below and just above: 2.0 kΩ and 2.2 kΩ.
  3. Pick the one with the smaller difference. If it's exactly in the middle, it's a tie, covered in Part B.

Topic 5 · putting it together

The design recipe: two loops, two resistors

The tool is Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL): around any closed loop, the voltage rises equal the voltage drops. Think of the supply's volts as a budget that each part spends.

The driver has two loops. Each loop has one unknown resistor.

Collector loop → R2

5 V supply → R2 → LED → collector to emitter → ground.

\[V_{CC} = I_C R_2 + V_{LED} + V_{CE}\]\[R_2 = \frac{V_{CC} - V_{LED} - V_{CE}}{I_C}\]
Base loop → R1

Pin → R1 → base to emitter → ground.

\[V_{in} = I_B R_1 + V_{BE}\]\[R_1 = \frac{V_{in} - V_{BE}}{I_B}, \quad I_B = \frac{I_C}{\beta_{min}}\]

The homework's approximations make these one-liners: \(V_{CE} = 0\) (saturated), \(V_{BE} = 0.7\) V, \(\beta_{min} = 10\), \(I_C = 20\) mA, and the LED's \(V_F\).

Watch out. \(I_B\) comes from \(\beta_{min} = 10\), never from the datasheet β. Using β = 300 gives a base resistor about 30 times too big, and the LED barely glows.

Topic 6 · simulation

LTspice, model cards and the DC operating point

LTspice is a free circuit simulator. You draw a schematic, tell it which analysis to run, and it solves the circuit with detailed device models instead of 0.7 V rules. Three ideas cover this homework.

1. Models. A generic "NPN" or "LED" symbol has no real behaviour until you name a model. Changing the transistor's value to 2N3904 picks a model from LTspice's built-in library. The White LED isn't built in, so you paste its model card as a SPICE directive (the .op button on the toolbar opens that window) and change the LED's value from D to WhiteLED so it uses the card.

Decode the White LED model card · click a piece

Solid pieces shape the DC answer. Dashed ones only matter when signals change quickly. From IS and N alone, \(V_F = N V_T \ln(I/I_S) = 6.45 \times 0.0259 \times \ln\!\big(0.02/93.1\text{p}\big) \approx 3.20\) V at 20 mA, where \(V_T \approx 25.9\) mV is the thermal voltage at 27 °C. That's where the homework's 3.2 V comes from.

2. The DC operating point (.op). Under Simulate › Configure Analysis (older versions: Edit Simulation Cmd), choose DC op pnt. LTspice then finds every steady DC voltage and current, with inductors treated as wires and capacitors as gaps. It prints a list like this:

--- Operating Point ---
V(vce):      volts       voltage   ← your net label
V(n001):     volts       voltage   ← unnamed nodes get numbers
Ic(Q1):      amps        device_current
Ib(Q1):      amps        device_current
Ie(Q1):      amps        device_current
I(R1):       amps        device_current

3. Net labels. A net label names a wire. Label Q1's collector Vce and the printout shows V(vce), its voltage to ground. Since the emitter is grounded, that is \(V_{CE}\).

Why won't the simulation match your hand numbers exactly? The model is more honest than the rules of thumb:

  • \(V_{BE}\) comes out near 0.78 V, a bit above 0.7 V. 2 mA is a lot of base current for a small transistor.
  • \(V_{CE}\) is a few tens of millivolts, close to 0 V without being exactly 0.
  • The LED's voltage is set by its exponential curve, very close to 3.2 V at 20 mA.

So expect \(I_C\) near your design value, \(I_B\) a little under 2 mA, and \(I_C/I_B\) near 10, the forced beta you designed in. That's far below the 2N3904's β of about 300.

Go deeper: SPICE number suffixes

SPICE ignores case and reads these suffixes: f = 10⁻¹⁵, p = 10⁻¹², n = 10⁻⁹, u = 10⁻⁶, m = 10⁻³, k = 10³, meg = 10⁶, g = 10⁹. So RS=42M is 42 milliohms. To write 42 megohms you'd type 42meg. It's the most common SPICE surprise.

The pnp LED driver

The big idea

A pnp is an npn turned upside down. Its emitter sits on +5 V, a LOW pin pulls current out of its base to turn it on, and the LED hangs below it on the way to ground.

Topic 7 · mirror image

The pnp transistor: everything flipped

A pnp works like an npn with every voltage and current reversed:

Writing \(V_{EB}\) and \(V_{EC}\) keeps the numbers positive, since the emitter is the high side.

Analogy · a high-side switch

The npn is a switch on the ground side of the load: it connects the bottom of the LED to ground. The pnp is a switch on the supply side: it connects the top of the LED to +5 V. Same job, opposite end of the load.

pnp driver · sweep the pin · example values

Sweep the pin from 0 V up to 5 V. The LED only goes dark once the pin gets within about 0.6 V of the emitter. beyond the homework Look at the 3.3 V marker. A 3.3 V microcontroller's HIGH can't turn this pnp off, because 5 − 3.3 = 1.7 V still forward-biases its base. That's why the homework drives it with a 5 V signal.

pnp: emitter to +V, pull the base LOW to turn it on, and the pin must reach the full supply to turn it off.

Topic 8 · same recipe, flipped loops

Designing the pnp driver, and what to do with a tie

The same two loops, each starting at +5 V:

Collector loop → R2

+5 V → emitter → collector → LED → R2 → ground.

\[V_{CC} = V_{EC} + V_{LED} + I_C R_2\]
Base loop → R1

+5 V → emitter → base → R1 → pin at 0 V.

\[V_{CC} = V_{EB} + I_B R_1 + V_{in}\]

A tie in rounding. Sometimes your value lands exactly halfway between two E24 values by plain subtraction. The homework doesn't say what to do, so here are three sensible ways to break it:

  • Round half up, the usual school rule, picks the larger value.
  • Compare ratios. E24 is spaced by ratio, so compare the percentage error to each side. A value halfway by subtraction is slightly closer to the upper neighbour by ratio.
  • Pick the safe side. A larger LED resistor gives slightly less current, which is gentler on the LED and on the pin.

All three pick the larger one. Either neighbour should be accepted, though. Problem 4's reveal shows the simulation for both.

Watch out. The base loop starts at the 5 V emitter. The pin's 0 V is the end of the loop, so R1 drops 5 − 0.7 − 0 = 4.3 V. Using 3.3 V or forgetting the supply is the common slip.

Topic 9 · LTspice signs

Reading a pnp operating point

Part C · Problems 5–6 + extra credit · 9 + 3 pts

The npn relay driver

The big idea

Swap the LED for a relay coil on 12 V. The transistor switches the coil, the coil's magnet flips a separate switch, and that switch runs a 120 V lamp the microcontroller never touches. A diode across the coil catches the voltage spike when it turns off.

Topic 10 · from first principles

What a relay is

A relay is a switch flipped by a magnet. Inside:

  • A coil of fine copper wire around an iron core. Current through it makes an electromagnet.
  • A hinged iron armature that the magnet pulls down.
  • A spring that pulls the armature back when the coil lets go.
  • Contacts on the armature that close (or open) a completely separate circuit.

The coil side and the contact side share no wire. That isolation is the point: 3.3 V logic on one side, 120 V mains on the other.

Inside a relay · an example 12 V relay
magnet pull vs spring

Raise the voltage slowly. Nothing happens until about 8 V, the pull-in voltage, when the magnet beats the spring and the armature snaps shut. Now lower it. The contacts stay closed down to about 2.5 V, the drop-out voltage, because the closed gap makes the magnet much stronger. This example relay has a 400 Ω coil. The homework's has 800 Ω.

Analogy · a fridge magnet

Hold a magnet near the fridge and you feel a weak pull. Once it touches, it holds on hard. A relay's armature is the same: hard to pull in across a gap, easy to hold once closed. That's why it snaps cleanly instead of buzzing in between.

Topic 11 · the load is a resistor (mostly)

The coil at DC is just its resistance

A coil is an inductor (symbol L) made of a long, thin wire, which has resistance. Inductors only push back on changes in current:

\[v_L = L\,\frac{di}{dt}\]

Once the current is steady, \(di/dt = 0\) and the inductance drops out. All that's left is the wire's resistance, so a relay datasheet lists a coil resistance, 800 Ω here. At DC:

\[I_{coil} = \frac{V_{CC} - V_{CE}}{R_{coil}}\]

With the transistor saturated, \(V_{CE} \approx 0\), so the coil gets the full supply. That current is the saturation current \(I_{C,sat}\), the most the collector loop allows. It plays the role the 20 mA LED current played in Part A. After that, the same base recipe applies: \(I_B = I_{C,sat}/\beta_{min}\), then KVL for R1.

Watch out. The coil's 12 V comes from its own supply. The pin's 3.3 V only appears in the base loop. Mixing them up is the classic slip here.

Topic 13 · why D1 is there

The flyback kick, and the diode that catches it

An inductor stores energy in its magnetic field while current flows:

\[E = \tfrac12 L I^2\]

It also refuses to let its current change suddenly. From \(v = L\,di/dt\), stopping the current in an instant would take an infinite voltage. When Q1 switches off in a microsecond, the coil tries to keep its current flowing anyway, and the collector voltage shoots up until something gives. Without protection it can reach hundreds or thousands of volts. A 2N3904 is rated for only 40 V from collector to emitter, so it breaks down, and the energy can also spark across the switch.

D1 is a flyback diode (also called a freewheeling, catch or snubber diode). It sits across the coil with its cathode on +12 V:

  • Coil on: the bottom of the coil is near 0 V, so D1 is reverse-biased and does nothing.
  • Coil switched off: the coil's current pushes the bottom end up. As soon as it passes 12 V plus one diode drop, D1 turns on, and the coil current loops round through D1.
  • The collector is clamped near 12.7 V. The stored energy burns off as heat in the coil's resistance and the diode over a millisecond or so.

Analogy · water hammer

Slam a tap shut and the moving water in the pipe bangs, sometimes hard enough to burst it. The water's momentum is the coil's current. D1 is a bypass loop that lets the water keep circling until it slows on its own.

Inductor + sudden switch-off = voltage spike. A diode across the coil gives the current somewhere to go.

Cheat sheet

npn low-side driver
\(R_2 = \dfrac{V_{CC} - V_{LED} - V_{CE}}{I_C}\)
\(R_1 = \dfrac{V_{in} - V_{BE}}{I_C/\beta_{min}}\)

Pin HIGH = on. Emitter to ground. For a relay, drop \(V_{LED}\) and use the coil resistance: \(I_{C,sat} = V_{CC}/R_{coil}\).

pnp high-side driver
\(R_2 = \dfrac{V_{CC} - V_{EC} - V_{LED}}{I_C}\)
\(R_1 = \dfrac{V_{CC} - V_{EB} - V_{in}}{I_C/\beta_{min}}\)

Pin LOW = on. Emitter to +V. The pin must reach \(V_{CC}\) to turn it off.

Switch rules of thumb
\(V_{BE} \approx 0.7\) V · \(V_{CE(sat)} \approx 0\)
\(\beta_{min} = 10\)

Saturation: the load sets \(I_C\), and \(I_C/I_B\) falls well below β. Simulation gives \(V_{BE}\) of 0.77 to 0.78 V and \(V_{CE}\) of a few tens of mV.

Inductors & flyback
\(v = L\,\dfrac{di}{dt}\) · \(E = \tfrac12 LI^2\)

At DC a coil is its resistance. At switch-off it spikes. A reverse diode across the coil, cathode to +V, clamps it.

Common mistakes

  • Using the datasheet β (100–300) instead of \(\beta_{min} = 10\) for \(I_B\). The base resistor comes out far too big and the transistor never saturates.
  • Forgetting \(V_{BE}\) in the base loop: \(R_1 = 3.3/I_B\) instead of \((3.3 - 0.7)/I_B\).
  • In the pnp base loop, starting from the pin instead of the 5 V emitter. R1 drops \(5 - 0.7 - V_{in}\).
  • Rounding to a value that isn't in E24, like 1.7 kΩ, or rounding to the wrong decade.
  • Reading pnp currents' minus signs as an error, or reporting \(V_C\) where the table asks for \(V_{EC} = 5 - V_C\).
  • Typing RS=42M and thinking it means megohms. It means milliohms.
  • Using the pin's 3.3 V in the relay's collector loop. The coil runs from its own 12 V.
  • Leaving out the flyback diode on a real relay circuit. The first switch-off can kill the transistor.
