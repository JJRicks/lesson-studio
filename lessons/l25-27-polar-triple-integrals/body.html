Lesson 25 · round regions

Double integrals in polar coordinates

The big idea

When a region is round, describe it with a distance and an angle. The limits become plain numbers. The one thing you have to remember is that the tiny area \(dA\) turns into \(r\,dr\,d\theta\).

Topic 1 · from first principles

Polar coordinates: another way to name a point

Normally you name a point by walking \(x\) steps across and \(y\) steps up. Polar coordinates name the same point by turning and walking:

  1. Face along the positive \(x\)-axis.
  2. Turn counterclockwise by an angle \(\theta\) (the Greek letter theta), measured in radians.
  3. Walk forward a distance \(r\).
Analogy · a radar screen

A radar operator doesn't report "three miles east, four miles north." They report a bearing and a range. The bearing is \(\theta\) and the range is \(r\).

The walk forms a right triangle with hypotenuse \(r\), so trigonometry gives the conversions:

Polar to rectangular

\[x = r\cos\theta,\qquad y = r\sin\theta\]

Rectangular to polar

\[r^2 = x^2 + y^2,\qquad \tan\theta = \frac{y}{x}\]

Here's the payoff. The circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 4\) is a messy square-root curve in \(x\) and \(y\). In polar it's \(r = 2\).

The polar rectangle

In \(xy\) coordinates the easiest region is a rectangle, with \(x\) between two numbers and \(y\) between two numbers. A polar rectangle uses the same recipe with \(r\) and \(\theta\):

\[R = \{(r,\theta)\ \mid\ a \le r \le b,\ \ \alpha \le \theta \le \beta\}\]

On paper it looks like a slice of a ring. Its name comes from its sides, which each hold \(r\) or \(\theta\) constant. Two of the sides are arcs (\(r = a\) and \(r = b\)) and two are straight rays (\(\theta = \alpha\) and \(\theta = \beta\)).

Topic 2 · where the factor comes from

Why \(dA\) becomes \(r\,dr\,d\theta\)

Cut a polar rectangle with circles spaced \(\Delta r\) apart and rays spaced \(\Delta\theta\) apart. You get small cells. All of them have the same \(\Delta r\) and \(\Delta\theta\). They do not all have the same area.

Analogy · cutting a pizza

Cut a pizza along rings and along slice lines. The pieces near the center are tiny. The pieces at the crust are large, even though each one spans the same angle and the same ring width. Being farther out gives you more crust for the same angle.

To find the area of one cell, start from what a radian is:

  1. Arc length

    On a circle of radius \(r\), an angle \(\Delta\theta\) cuts off an arc of length \(r\,\Delta\theta\). The farther out, the longer the arc.

  2. The cell is almost a trapezoid

    Zoom in and the cell has two nearly parallel arcs, of lengths \(r_{i-1}\Delta\theta\) and \(r_i\Delta\theta\), a distance \(\Delta r\) apart.

  3. Trapezoid area

    Width times the average of the parallel sides:

    \[\Delta A = \tfrac12\,\Delta r\,\big(r_{i-1}\Delta\theta + r_i\Delta\theta\big)\]
  4. Name the middle radius

    Let \(r^* = \tfrac12(r_{i-1} + r_i)\). Factoring gives the result:

    \[\Delta A = r^*\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\]

The \(r\) is a stretch factor. It says how much bigger a real cell is than its \(\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\) would suggest.

The double integral over a polar rectangle

Stand a thin box on each cell, tall enough to reach the surface \(z = f(r,\theta)\). One box has volume height × base, which is \(f(r^*,\theta^*)\,r^*\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\). Add all the boxes and make the grid finer:

Double integral over a polar rectangle \[\iint_R f(r,\theta)\,dA = \lim_{m,n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n} f(r^*_{ij},\theta^*_{ij})\,r^*_{ij}\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta = \int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\!\!\int_{a}^{b} f(r,\theta)\,r\,dr\,d\theta\]

Things to notice

  • \(dA\) is replaced with \(r\,dr\,d\theta\) every time.
  • Every property of double integrals from rectangular coordinates still holds, so use them.
  • To convert an integrand, substitute \(x = r\cos\theta\) and \(y = r\sin\theta\).
Topic 3 · the method

Evaluating over a polar rectangle

  1. Describe the region

    Write down the numbers that bound \(r\) and \(\theta\).

  2. Convert the integrand

    Replace \(x\) and \(y\). Then replace \(dA\) with \(r\,dr\,d\theta\).

  3. Integrate \(r\) first

    Treat \(\theta\) as a constant.

  4. Integrate \(\theta\)

    What's left depends on \(\theta\) only.

Count your \(r\)'s. The \(r\) from \(dA\) is added to whatever \(r\)'s the function already has. If \(f\) contains one \(r\), the integrand ends up with \(r^2\).
Example 1Polar rectanglenotes p. 4

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\iint_R 2\theta r\,dA\) over the region \(R = \left\{(r,\theta)\ \middle|\ 1 \le r \le 7,\ \tfrac{2\pi}{3} \le \theta \le \tfrac{3\pi}{2}\right\}\).

  1. Read the region

    Every bound is a constant, so this is a polar rectangle. It's a slice of a ring running from \(120^\circ\) around to \(270^\circ\).

  2. Replace \(dA\)

    The \(r\) in \(2\theta r\) belongs to the function. \(dA\) brings a second one:

    \[\int_{2\pi/3}^{3\pi/2}\!\!\int_1^7 2\theta r\cdot r\,dr\,d\theta = \int_{2\pi/3}^{3\pi/2}\!\!\int_1^7 2\theta r^2\,dr\,d\theta\]
  3. Inner integral (\(\theta\) is a constant)
    \[\int_1^7 2\theta r^2\,dr = 2\theta\left[\frac{r^3}{3}\right]_1^7 = 2\theta\cdot\frac{342}{3} = 228\,\theta\]
  4. Outer integral
    \[\int_{2\pi/3}^{3\pi/2} 228\,\theta\,d\theta = 114\Big[\theta^2\Big]_{2\pi/3}^{3\pi/2}\]
  5. Plug in the angles

    \(\left(\tfrac{3\pi}{2}\right)^2 - \left(\tfrac{2\pi}{3}\right)^2 = \tfrac{9\pi^2}{4} - \tfrac{4\pi^2}{9} = \tfrac{65\pi^2}{36}\). Multiply by 114.

Reveal the answer
\(\dfrac{1235\pi^2}{6} \approx 2031.49\)\(114 \cdot \tfrac{65\pi^2}{36}\), reduced.

If you got about 428 instead, you used only one \(r\). The second one matters a lot on a region reaching out to \(r = 7\).

Example 2Convert from \(x\) and \(y\)notes p. 5

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\iint_R (x+y)\,dA\) where \(R = \{(x,y)\ |\ 1 \le x^2 + y^2 \le 4,\ x \le 0\}\).

  1. Convert the region

    \(1 \le r^2 \le 4\) means \(1 \le r \le 2\). The condition \(x \le 0\) keeps the left half of the plane, so \(\tfrac{\pi}{2} \le \theta \le \tfrac{3\pi}{2}\).

  2. Convert the integrand

    \(x + y = r\cos\theta + r\sin\theta\), and \(dA = r\,dr\,d\theta\):

    \[\int_{\pi/2}^{3\pi/2}\!\!\int_1^2 r^2(\cos\theta + \sin\theta)\,dr\,d\theta\]
  3. Inner integral

    The trig part is a constant while \(r\) changes, and \(\int_1^2 r^2\,dr = \tfrac{7}{3}\). That leaves \(\tfrac73(\cos\theta + \sin\theta)\).

  4. Outer integral
    \[\frac73\int_{\pi/2}^{3\pi/2}(\cos\theta + \sin\theta)\,d\theta = \frac73\Big[\sin\theta - \cos\theta\Big]_{\pi/2}^{3\pi/2}\]
  5. Evaluate at the ends

    At \(\tfrac{3\pi}{2}\): \(-1 - 0 = -1\). At \(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\): \(1 - 0 = 1\). The bracket is \(-2\). Multiply by \(\tfrac73\).

Reveal the answer
\(-\dfrac{14}{3} \approx -4.667\)\(\tfrac73 \cdot (-2)\).

Negative makes sense. The region sits where \(x\) is negative, so the plane \(z = x + y\) dips below the floor over more of it than it rises above.

Quick check (notes Practice 1): \(\iint_R 7y\,dA\) over \(36 \le x^2 + y^2 \le 81,\ y \le 0\).

\(6 \le r \le 9\), and \(y \le 0\) means \(\pi \le \theta \le 2\pi\). The integral is \(\int_\pi^{2\pi}\!\int_6^9 7r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta = 7\cdot 171\cdot(-2) = -2394\), matching the notes.

Topic 4

General polar regions

Most round regions aren't perfect ring slices. Their edges are curves like a cardioid. This is the same step you took from rectangles to Type I and Type II regions: the inner limits become functions.

General polar region

If \(D = \{(r,\theta)\ |\ \alpha \le \theta \le \beta,\ h_1(\theta) \le r \le h_2(\theta)\}\) and \(f\) is continuous on \(D\), then

\[\iint_D f(r,\theta)\,r\,dr\,d\theta = \int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\!\!\int_{h_1(\theta)}^{h_2(\theta)} f(r,\theta)\,r\,dr\,d\theta.\]
Analogy · a lighthouse beam

Stand at the origin with a lighthouse beam. Point it at angle \(\theta\). The beam enters the region at distance \(h_1(\theta)\) and leaves at \(h_2(\theta)\). Those are your inner limits. The first and last angles where the beam touches the region are your outer limits.

Beam explorer

Move the beam. The glowing part is the piece of the beam inside the region. Its two ends are the inner limits at that angle.

The line starts at the origin and shoots outward. Where it enters and leaves gives the inner limits.

Example 3Cardioid regionnotes p. 7

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\iint_D r^2\sin(\theta)\,r\,dr\,d\theta\) where \(D\) is the region bounded by the polar axis and the upper half of the cardioid \(r = 1 + \cos\theta\).

  1. Find the limits

    A ray from the origin starts inside at \(r = 0\) and leaves through the cardioid at \(r = 1 + \cos\theta\). The upper half runs \(0 \le \theta \le \pi\).

  2. Write the integral

    The \(r\) from \(dA\) is already written in the problem, so don't add another.

    \[\int_0^{\pi}\!\!\int_0^{1+\cos\theta} r^3\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\]
  3. Inner integral
    \[\sin\theta\left[\frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^{1+\cos\theta} = \frac14\sin\theta\,(1+\cos\theta)^4\]
  4. Substitute

    Let \(u = 1 + \cos\theta\), so \(du = -\sin\theta\,d\theta\). At \(\theta = 0\), \(u = 2\). At \(\theta = \pi\), \(u = 0\). Flipping the limits absorbs the minus sign.

    \[\frac14\int_0^{2} u^4\,du\]
  5. Integrate

    \(\tfrac14\left[\tfrac{u^5}{5}\right]_0^2\). Finish the arithmetic.

Reveal the answer
\(\dfrac{8}{5} = 1.6\)\(\tfrac14\cdot\tfrac{32}{5}\).
Topic 5

Polar volumes

A double integral adds up (height) × (little area). If the height is a surface \(z = f(r,\theta)\), the total is the volume under it.

Volume under a surface \[V = \iint_R f(r,\theta)\,r\,dr\,d\theta = \int_\alpha^\beta\!\!\int_a^b f(r,\theta)\,r\,dr\,d\theta\]

Over a general base, replace \(a\) and \(b\) with \(h_1(\theta)\) and \(h_2(\theta)\).

Look out for \(x^2 + y^2\) in a problem. It's a strong hint to use polar, because it turns straight into \(r^2\).

Example 4Volumenotes p. 8

Use polar coordinates to find the volume of the solid bounded by the paraboloid \(z = -9x^2 - 9y^2 + 2\) and the planes \(x = 0\), \(y = 0\) and \(z = 0\).

  1. Picture it

    \(z = 2 - 9(x^2 + y^2)\) is a dome with its top at \(z = 2\). The planes \(x = 0\) and \(y = 0\) slice it into quarters. We'll use the first-quadrant quarter. All four are mirror images, so any of them gives the same volume.

  2. Find where the dome meets the floor

    Set \(z = 0\): \(0 = 2 - 9r^2\), so \(r = \tfrac{\sqrt2}{3}\).

  3. Set up in polar
    \[V = \int_0^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^{\sqrt2/3} (2 - 9r^2)\,r\,dr\,d\theta\]
  4. Inner integral
    \[\int_0^{\sqrt2/3}(2r - 9r^3)\,dr = \left[r^2 - \frac{9r^4}{4}\right]_0^{\sqrt2/3} = \frac29 - \frac19 = \frac19\]
  5. Outer integral

    \(\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac19\,d\theta\). The integrand is constant, so multiply by the width of the interval.

Reveal the answer
\(V = \dfrac{\pi}{18} \approx 0.1745\) units³
Quick check (notes Practice 3): the volume under the cone \(z = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) above the disk \(3x^2 + 3y^2 \le 4\).

\(z = r\) and \(0 \le r \le \tfrac{2}{\sqrt3}\), so \(V = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^{2/\sqrt3} r\cdot r\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi\cdot\tfrac13\left(\tfrac{2}{\sqrt3}\right)^3 = \tfrac{16\pi\sqrt3}{27}\), matching the notes.

Topic 6

Polar areas

Put a height of 1 everywhere. A solid of height 1 has the same number for its volume as its base has for its area. That gives an area formula:

Area in polar \[A = \int_\alpha^\beta\!\!\int_{h_1(\theta)}^{h_2(\theta)} 1\,r\,dr\,d\theta\]

Sketching the curve is what keeps you honest about the limits. Polar curves have one quirk worth learning. \(r\) can come out negative. When it does, you walk backward through the origin, so the point lands on the opposite side from the angle \(\theta\).

Example 5Area of a rosenotes p. 9

Find the area of the region \(D\) bounded by the polar curve \(r = -6\sin(4\theta)\), where \(0 \le \theta \le \tfrac{3\pi}{2}\).

  1. Write the area integral

    Rays leave the origin at \(r = 0\) and stop at the curve. Call the curve \(h(\theta) = -6\sin 4\theta\).

    \[A = \int_0^{3\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^{h(\theta)} r\,dr\,d\theta\]
  2. Deal with negative \(r\)

    \(h\) is negative for some angles. That just means that petal is drawn on the far side of the origin. The next step squares \(h\), so every petal adds positive area.

  3. Inner integral
    \[\int_0^{h} r\,dr = \frac{h^2}{2} = \frac{36\sin^2 4\theta}{2} = 18\sin^2 4\theta\]
  4. Reduce the power

    Use \(\sin^2 u = \tfrac12(1 - \cos 2u)\) with \(u = 4\theta\). The integrand becomes \(9 - 9\cos 8\theta\).

  5. Outer integral
    \[\int_0^{3\pi/2}(9 - 9\cos 8\theta)\,d\theta = \left[9\theta - \frac98\sin 8\theta\right]_0^{3\pi/2}\]

    \(\sin(12\pi) = 0\), so only one term survives.

Reveal the answer
\(A = \dfrac{27\pi}{2} \approx 42.41\) units²\(9\cdot\tfrac{3\pi}{2}\).

Check it by petals. Each petal uses an angle span of \(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\), so \(\tfrac{3\pi}{2}\) traces six of the rose's eight petals, and none overlap. One petal has area \(\tfrac{9\pi}{4}\), and \(6\cdot\tfrac{9\pi}{4}\) gives the same total.

Quick check (notes Practice 2): the area in Quadrant I bounded by the rose \(r = 8\sin 2\theta\).

One petal, \(0 \le \theta \le \tfrac{\pi}{2}\): \(\int_0^{\pi/2}\tfrac12\cdot 64\sin^2 2\theta\,d\theta = 32\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{4} = 8\pi\), matching the notes.

Lesson 26 · one more dimension

Triple integrals

The big idea

Chop a solid into tiny boxes. Multiply each box's volume by the value of \(f\) there. Add them up. You compute that by integrating one variable at a time, from the inside out.

Topic 1 · from first principles

What a triple integral means

A function \(f(x,y,z)\) takes a point in space and gives back one number. Graphing it would take four dimensions, so stop picturing height. Picture a value living at every point of a solid, like a color.

Analogy · a block of uneven clay

Suppose \(f\) is density, and the clay is packed tighter in some spots. To weigh the block, cut it into tiny cubes. Each cube's mass is about density × volume. Add every cube. That total is the triple integral.

Here's the setup. Take a box \(B = \{a \le x \le b,\ c \le y \le d,\ e \le z \le f\}\). Divide the \(x\) range into \(l\) pieces, the \(y\) range into \(m\) and the \(z\) range into \(n\). You get \(lmn\) sub-boxes, each with volume \(\Delta V = \Delta x\,\Delta y\,\Delta z\). (The \(f\) in those bounds is a number, separate from the function \(f\). The notes reuse the letter.)

Triple integral \[\iiint_B f(x,y,z)\,dV = \lim_{l,m,n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{l}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{k=1}^{n} f(x^*_{ijk},y^*_{ijk},z^*_{ijk})\,\Delta x\,\Delta y\,\Delta z\]

When this limit exists, \(f\) is integrable on \(B\). It always exists if \(f\) is continuous on \(B\).

Topic 2 · how to compute one

Fubini's theorem: work from the inside out

You never compute that limit directly. You integrate one variable at a time. While you integrate one, the others stand still as constants.

Fubini's theorem for triple integrals

If \(f\) is continuous on the box \(B = [a,b]\times[c,d]\times[e,f]\), then

\[\iiint_B f(x,y,z)\,dV = \int_e^f\!\!\int_c^d\!\!\int_a^b f(x,y,z)\,dx\,dy\,dz,\]

and the other five orders of integration give the same value.

Typo in the notes. They print the box as \([a,b]\times[c,e]\times[e,f]\). The middle interval should be \([c,d]\), which is what the integral's limits show.
Analogy · stacking sugar cubes

Line cubes up into a stick. Lay sticks side by side into a sheet. Stack sheets into a block. Each integral does one of those moves: stick, sheet, block.

The order of the differentials says which comes first. In \(dx\,dy\,dz\), the \(x\) integral is innermost, so you do it first. The limits nearest the integrand go with the first differential.

Shortcut: if the limits are all constants and the integrand is a product like \(g(x)\,h(y)\,k(z)\), the triple integral splits into three separate single integrals multiplied together.

Example 1Inside outnotes p. 4

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_2^4\!\!\int_1^5 \left(x + yz^2\right)dx\,dy\,dz\).

  1. Read the order

    \(dx\,dy\,dz\): \(x\) first (1 to 5), then \(y\) (2 to 4), then \(z\) (0 to 1).

  2. Integrate \(x\) (\(y\) and \(z\) are constants)
    \[\int_1^5 (x + yz^2)\,dx = \left[\frac{x^2}{2} + xyz^2\right]_1^5 = 12 + 4yz^2\]
  3. Integrate \(y\) (\(z\) is a constant)
    \[\int_2^4 (12 + 4yz^2)\,dy = \Big[12y + 2y^2z^2\Big]_2^4 = 24 + 24z^2\]
  4. Integrate \(z\)

    \(\displaystyle\int_0^1 (24 + 24z^2)\,dz = \Big[24z + 8z^3\Big]_0^1\). Evaluate it.

Reveal the answer
\(32\)\(24 + 8\).
Example 2Splits into a productnotes p. 4

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_{-3}^{-2}\!\!\int_1^2\!\!\int_0^{\ln 3} y^2z^3e^{2x}\,dx\,dy\,dz\).

  1. Spot the product

    The integrand is a function of \(x\) times one of \(y\) times one of \(z\), and all limits are constants. So it splits:

    \[\int_0^{\ln 3} e^{2x}\,dx\ \cdot\ \int_1^2 y^2\,dy\ \cdot\ \int_{-3}^{-2} z^3\,dz\]
  2. The \(x\) factor

    \(\tfrac12\big[e^{2x}\big]_0^{\ln 3} = \tfrac12(9 - 1) = 4\). Remember that \(e^{2\ln 3} = e^{\ln 9} = 9\).

  3. The \(y\) factor

    \(\left[\tfrac{y^3}{3}\right]_1^2 = \tfrac{8}{3} - \tfrac13 = \tfrac73\).

  4. The \(z\) factor

    \(\left[\tfrac{z^4}{4}\right]_{-3}^{-2} = \tfrac{16 - 81}{4} = -\tfrac{65}{4}\). Negative is right, since \(z^3\) is negative on this interval.

  5. Multiply

    \(4 \cdot \tfrac73 \cdot \left(-\tfrac{65}{4}\right)\). The 4's cancel.

Reveal the answer
\(-\dfrac{455}{3} \approx -151.67\)
Quick check (notes Practice 1): \(\displaystyle\int_1^2\!\int_2^3\!\int_1^3 \frac{7yz^2}{5x^3}\,dx\,dy\,dz\).

It splits: \(\tfrac75\cdot\int_1^3 x^{-3}\,dx\cdot\int_2^3 y\,dy\cdot\int_1^2 z^2\,dz = \tfrac75\cdot\tfrac49\cdot\tfrac52\cdot\tfrac73 = \tfrac{98}{27}\), matching the notes.

Topic 3

Triple integrals over general solids

Real solids aren't boxes. To describe one, look at its shadow on the floor. Call the shadow \(D\). Above each point of the shadow, the solid runs from a bottom surface \(z = u_1(x,y)\) to a top surface \(z = u_2(x,y)\):

Triple integral over a general region

If \(E = \{(x,y,z)\ |\ (x,y) \in D,\ u_1(x,y) \le z \le u_2(x,y)\}\) and \(f\) is continuous on \(E\), then

\[\iiint_E f(x,y,z)\,dV = \iint_D\left[\int_{u_1(x,y)}^{u_2(x,y)} f(x,y,z)\,dz\right]dA.\]

So the job comes down to three questions, asked in order:

  1. Stick in the \(z\) direction

    Push a vertical stick up through the solid. It enters through \(z = u_1(x,y)\) and leaves through \(z = u_2(x,y)\).

  2. Line in the \(y\) direction

    Flatten the solid to its shadow. Draw a line across the shadow in the \(y\) direction. It enters at \(y = g_1(x)\) and leaves at \(y = g_2(x)\).

  3. Range of \(x\)

    See where \(x\) starts and stops: \(x = a\) to \(x = b\). These are plain numbers.

\[\iiint_E f\,dV = \int_a^b\!\!\int_{g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)}\!\!\int_{u_1(x,y)}^{u_2(x,y)} f(x,y,z)\,dz\,dy\,dx\]

Example 3Tetrahedronnotes p. 7

Evaluate the triple integral of \(f(x,y,z) = 5x - 3y\) over the solid tetrahedron bounded by the planes \(x = 0\), \(y = 0\), \(z = 0\) and \(x + y + z = 1\).

  1. Stick in \(z\)

    It enters through the floor at \(z = 0\) and leaves through the slanted plane at \(z = 1 - x - y\).

  2. Line in \(y\) on the shadow

    The shadow is where the slanted plane hits the floor. Set \(z = 0\): \(x + y = 1\). So \(y\) runs from 0 to \(1 - x\).

  3. Range of \(x\)

    From 0 to 1.

    \[\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{1-x}\!\!\int_0^{1-x-y}(5x - 3y)\,dz\,dy\,dx\]
  4. \(z\) integral

    \(f\) has no \(z\) in it, so it just multiplies the stick length: \((5x - 3y)(1 - x - y)\).

  5. \(y\) integral

    Let \(a = 1 - x\) to keep it short. Then \(\int_0^a (5x - 3y)(a - y)\,dy = \tfrac52xa^2 - \tfrac12a^3\). Factoring gives

    \[\frac{(1-x)^2(6x - 1)}{2}\]
  6. \(x\) integral

    Substitute \(u = 1 - x\), so \(6x - 1 = 5 - 6u\):

    \[\frac12\int_0^1 u^2(5 - 6u)\,du = \frac12\left[\frac{5u^3}{3} - \frac{3u^4}{2}\right]_0^1\]
Reveal the answer
\(\dfrac{1}{12}\)\(\tfrac12\left(\tfrac53 - \tfrac32\right)\).
Quick check (notes Practice 2): set up \(\iiint (9x + 4y)\,dV\) over the tetrahedron bounded by the coordinate planes and \(4x + 7y + 9z = 252\), integrating \(z\), then \(y\), then \(x\).

Solve for the top: \(z = 28 - \tfrac{4x}{9} - \tfrac{7y}{9}\). Set \(z = 0\): \(y = 36 - \tfrac{4x}{7}\). Set \(y = 0\) too: \(x = 63\). So \(\int_0^{63}\!\int_0^{36 - 4x/7}\!\int_0^{28 - 4x/9 - 7y/9}(9x + 4y)\,dz\,dy\,dx\), matching the notes.

Topic 4

Applications and finding volume

What a triple integral measures depends on what \(f\) is.

\(f = 1\)

Adds up 1 × each tiny volume, which gives the volume of the solid.

\(f =\) density

Adds up density × tiny volume, which gives the total mass.

\(f =\) charge density

Gives the total charge.

Divide by volume

Gives the average value of \(f\) over the solid. That's the next topic.

\[V(E) = \iiint_E 1\,dV\]

With \(f = 1\), the \(z\) integral is just the length of the stick: top minus bottom.

Example 4Volumenotes p. 8

Find the volume of the solid \(D\) bounded above by the paraboloid \(z = 4x^2 + 4y^2 + 4\) and below by the \(xy\)-plane. The projection of \(D\) onto the \(xy\)-plane is bounded by the \(x\)- and \(y\)-axes and the lines \(x = 3\) and \(y = 2\).

  1. Set up \(\iiint 1\,dV\)

    Sticks run from the floor up to the paraboloid. The shadow is the rectangle \([0,3]\times[0,2]\).

    \[V = \int_0^3\!\!\int_0^2\!\!\int_0^{4x^2+4y^2+4} 1\,dz\,dy\,dx\]
  2. \(z\) integral

    The stick's length is \(4x^2 + 4y^2 + 4\).

  3. \(y\) integral
    \[\int_0^2 (4x^2 + 4y^2 + 4)\,dy = \left[4x^2y + \frac{4y^3}{3} + 4y\right]_0^2 = 8x^2 + \frac{56}{3}\]
  4. \(x\) integral

    \(\displaystyle\int_0^3\left(8x^2 + \frac{56}{3}\right)dx = \left[\frac{8x^3}{3} + \frac{56x}{3}\right]_0^3\). Evaluate it.

Reveal the answer
\(V = 128\) units³\(72 + 56\).
Topic 5

Average value of a function of three variables

The average of a list is its total divided by how many items there are. For a solid, the "total" is the triple integral and "how many" is the volume.

Average value \[f_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{V(E)}\iiint_E f(x,y,z)\,dV,\qquad V(E) = \iiint_E 1\,dV\]
Analogy · stirring the coffee

Pour hot coffee on one side of a cup and cold on the other. Stir until it's all one temperature. That final temperature is the average value of the temperature function.

Example 5Average temperaturenotes p. 9

The temperature at a point \((x,y,z)\) of a solid \(E\) bounded by the coordinate planes and the plane \(x + y + z = 1\) is \(T(x,y,z) = xy + 8x + 20\) degrees Celsius. Find the average temperature over the solid.

  1. Limits

    This is the same tetrahedron as Example 3, so the limits are the same: \(0 \le z \le 1 - x - y\), \(0 \le y \le 1 - x\), \(0 \le x \le 1\).

  2. Volume

    A tetrahedron is \(\tfrac13 \times\) base area \(\times\) height. The base triangle has area \(\tfrac12\) and the height is 1, so \(V = \tfrac16\).

  3. \(z\) integral

    \(T\) has no \(z\), so it multiplies the stick length: \((xy + 8x + 20)(1 - x - y)\).

  4. \(y\) integral

    With \(a = 1 - x\): \(\int_0^a (xy + 8x + 20)(a - y)\,dy = x\cdot\tfrac{a^3}{6} + (8x + 20)\cdot\tfrac{a^2}{2}\).

  5. \(x\) integral

    Substituting \(u = 1 - x\) turns each term into a plain polynomial:

    \[\int_0^1\left[\frac{x(1-x)^3}{6} + (4x + 10)(1-x)^2\right]dx = \frac{1}{120} + \frac13 + \frac{10}{3} = \frac{147}{40}\]
  6. Divide by the volume

    \(T_{\text{avg}} = \dfrac{147/40}{1/6}\). Dividing by \(\tfrac16\) is the same as multiplying by 6.

Reveal the answer
\(T_{\text{avg}} = \dfrac{441}{20} = 22.05\,{}^\circ\text{C}\)

This is close to 20, which makes sense. Most of a tetrahedron's volume sits near the corner where \(x\) and \(y\) are small.

Quick check (notes Practice 3): the average of \(f = x^2yz^2\) over the cube \([0,6]^3\).

It splits, and the average of a product of independent factors is the product of their averages. The average of \(x^2\) on \([0,6]\) is 12, of \(y\) is 3, of \(z^2\) is 12, so \(12\cdot 3\cdot 12 = 432\), matching the notes.

Lesson 27 · round solids

Cylindrical and spherical coordinates

The big idea

Match the coordinates to the solid. Things round about an axis use cylindrical \((r,\theta,z)\), with \(dV = r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta\). Things round about a point use spherical \((\rho,\theta,\phi)\), with \(dV = \rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\).

Topic 1 · review from first principles

Cylindrical coordinates

Take polar coordinates on the floor and add a height. A point is \((r,\theta,z)\):

  • \((r,\theta)\) are the polar coordinates of the point's shadow on the \(xy\)-plane.
  • \(z\) is the usual height.
Analogy · a seat on a carousel

Say where someone is on a carousel: how far from the center pole (\(r\)), how far around (\(\theta\)), and how high their horse is right now (\(z\)).

Converting \[x = r\cos\theta,\quad y = r\sin\theta,\quad z = z \qquad\qquad r^2 = x^2 + y^2,\quad \tan\theta = \frac{y}{x},\quad z = z\]

Hold one coordinate fixed and the rest of space traces out a surface:

  • \(r = c\) is an endless vertical cylinder around the \(z\)-axis.
  • \(\theta = \alpha\) is a vertical half-plane hinged on the \(z\)-axis, at angle \(\alpha\) from the positive \(x\)-axis.
  • \(z = m\) is a horizontal plane.
Correction to the notes. They say \(\theta = \alpha\) makes a constant angle \(\alpha\) "with the \(xy\)-plane." That surface actually stands straight up. The angle \(\alpha\) is measured around the \(z\)-axis, starting from the positive \(x\) direction.

Triple integrals in cylindrical coordinates

A cylindrical box is the polar rectangle from Lesson 25 with a height added: \(B = \{a \le r \le b,\ \alpha \le \theta \le \beta,\ c \le z \le d\}\). Chop it up and each tiny piece is a polar cell of area \(r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\) with height \(\Delta z\).

\[\Delta V = r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta z\]

Same \(\Delta r\), \(\Delta\theta\) and \(\Delta z\) the whole time. Only the arc changes, and it's proportional to \(r\).

Fubini's theorem in cylindrical coordinates

If \(g(x,y,z)\) is continuous on a cylindrical box \(B\), then with \(f(r,\theta,z) = g(r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta, z)\),

\[\iiint_B g(x,y,z)\,dV = \int_c^d\!\!\int_\alpha^\beta\!\!\int_a^b f(r,\theta,z)\,r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz.\]

Any of the other five orders gives the same value.

Cylindrical coordinates work well for solids that are symmetric around an axis, like cylinders and cones.

Example 1Cylindrical boxnotes p. 5

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\iiint_B (zr\sin\theta)\,r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz\) where \(B = \left\{(r,\theta,z)\ \middle|\ 0 \le r \le 2,\ 0 \le \theta \le \tfrac{\pi}{2},\ 0 \le z \le 4\right\}\).

  1. Read the solid

    A quarter of a cylinder, radius 2 and height 4. The second \(r\) is the \(dV\) factor, already written for you.

  2. Split it

    Constant limits and a product, so the integral splits:

    \[\int_0^4 z\,dz\ \cdot\ \int_0^{\pi/2}\sin\theta\,d\theta\ \cdot\ \int_0^2 r^2\,dr\]
  3. The \(r\) factor

    \(\left[\tfrac{r^3}{3}\right]_0^2 = \tfrac83\).

  4. The \(\theta\) factor

    \(\big[-\cos\theta\big]_0^{\pi/2} = 0 - (-1) = 1\).

  5. The \(z\) factor, then multiply

    \(\left[\tfrac{z^2}{2}\right]_0^4 = 8\). Multiply the three results.

Reveal the answer
\(\dfrac{64}{3} \approx 21.33\)\(8 \cdot 1 \cdot \tfrac83\).
Topic 3

General cylindrical solids

This is the same stick method from Lesson 26. Find the shadow \(D\) on the floor and describe it in polar. A vertical stick runs from a bottom surface \(u_1(r,\theta)\) to a top surface \(u_2(r,\theta)\).

General solid, cylindrical

For \(E = \{(r,\theta) \in D,\ u_1 \le z \le u_2\}\) with \(D = \{\alpha \le \theta \le \beta,\ g_1(\theta) \le r \le g_2(\theta)\}\),

\[\iiint_E f\,dV = \int_\alpha^\beta\!\!\int_{g_1(\theta)}^{g_2(\theta)}\!\!\int_{u_1(r,\theta)}^{u_2(r,\theta)} f(r,\theta,z)\,r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta.\]
Example 2Slanted topnotes p. 6

Given the region \(E = \{(x,y,z)\ |\ 0 \le x^2 + y^2 \le 4,\ x \ge 0,\ y \ge 0,\ 0 \le z \le x + 2\}\), convert the integral \(\displaystyle\iiint_E \frac{1}{x+2}\,dV\) into cylindrical coordinates and evaluate it.

  1. The shadow

    \(x^2 + y^2 \le 4\) with \(x, y \ge 0\) is a quarter disk: \(0 \le r \le 2\), \(0 \le \theta \le \tfrac{\pi}{2}\).

  2. The stick

    It runs from the floor to the slanted plane: \(0 \le z \le r\cos\theta + 2\).

  3. Set it up

    Replace \(x\), and use \(dV = r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta\):

    \[\int_0^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^2\!\!\int_0^{r\cos\theta+2} \frac{r}{r\cos\theta + 2}\,dz\,dr\,d\theta\]
  4. \(z\) integral

    There's no \(z\) in the integrand, so multiply by the stick length. The length is exactly the denominator, so they cancel:

    \[\frac{r}{r\cos\theta + 2}\cdot(r\cos\theta + 2) = r\]
  5. \(r\), then \(\theta\)

    \(\displaystyle\int_0^2 r\,dr = 2\), which leaves \(\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2} 2\,d\theta\).

Reveal the answer
\(\pi\)

The answer equals the area of the quarter disk. Each stick's weight canceled to 1, so adding sticks is the same as measuring the shadow.

Quick check (notes Practice 1): \(\iiint_E (5x^2 + 5y^2)\,dV\) over the quarter disk \(0 \le x^2+y^2 \le 4\), \(x, y \ge 0\), with \(0 \le z \le -x + 2\).

\(f = 5r^2\) and the stick length is \(2 - r\cos\theta\). So \(\int_0^2\!\int_0^{\pi/2} 5r^3(2 - r\cos\theta)\,d\theta\,dr = \int_0^2 5r^3(\pi - r)\,dr = 20\pi - 32\), matching the notes.

Topic 4 · review from first principles

Spherical coordinates

A point is named by one distance and two angles, \((\rho,\theta,\phi)\):

  • \(\rho\) (the Greek letter rho) is the straight-line distance from the origin.
  • \(\theta\) is the same around-the-axis angle as in cylindrical.
  • \(\phi\) (the Greek letter phi) is the angle down from the positive \(z\)-axis, with \(0 \le \phi \le \pi\).
Analogy · a globe

\(\theta\) is longitude, sweeping around the Earth's axis. \(\phi\) is how far you've come down from the North Pole. It's 0 at the pole, \(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) at the equator and \(\pi\) at the South Pole. \(\rho\) is how far you are from the center of the Earth.

Where do the conversions come from? Draw the line from the origin to the point. It makes the angle \(\phi\) with the \(z\)-axis, and it has length \(\rho\), so:

\[z = \rho\cos\phi,\qquad r = \rho\sin\phi\]

Here \(r\) is the shadow's distance from the origin. Put \(r = \rho\sin\phi\) into \(x = r\cos\theta\) and \(y = r\sin\theta\):

Converting \[x = \rho\sin\phi\cos\theta,\quad y = \rho\sin\phi\sin\theta,\quad z = \rho\cos\phi\] \[\rho^2 = x^2+y^2+z^2,\quad \tan\theta = \frac{y}{x},\quad \phi = \arccos\frac{z}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}}\]

Topic 5 · where the factor comes from

Why \(dV = \rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\)

A spherical box is \(B = \{a \le \rho \le b,\ \alpha \le \theta \le \beta,\ \gamma \le \phi \le \psi\}\). A tiny piece of it is almost a rectangular brick. Multiply its three edges:

Radial edge

Straight out from the origin: length \(\Delta\rho\).

Down-the-globe edge

An arc on a circle of radius \(\rho\): length \(\rho\,\Delta\phi\).

Around-the-globe edge

An arc on a circle of latitude. That circle has radius \(\rho\sin\phi\), so the length is \(\rho\sin\phi\,\Delta\theta\).

\[\Delta V = (\Delta\rho)(\rho\,\Delta\phi)(\rho\sin\phi\,\Delta\theta) = \rho^2\sin\phi\,\Delta\rho\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta\phi\]

Latitude circles shrink to a point at the poles. That is the \(\sin\phi\). Pieces far from the center get bigger in two directions. That is the \(\rho^2\).

Triple integral in spherical coordinates \[\iiint_B f\,dV = \lim_{l,m,n\to\infty}\sum\sum\sum f(\rho^*,\theta^*,\phi^*)(\rho^*)^2\sin\phi^*\,\Delta\rho\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta\phi = \int_\alpha^\beta\!\!\int_\gamma^\psi\!\!\int_a^b f\,\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\]

Example 3Spherical boxnotes p. 9

Evaluate the iterated triple integral \(\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^1 \rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\).

  1. Read the solid

    \(\rho\) from 0 to 1 fills a unit ball. \(\phi\) from 0 to \(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) stops at the equator. \(\theta\) goes all the way around. The integrand is only the \(dV\) factor, so this is a volume.

  2. \(\rho\) integral

    \(\sin\phi\) is a constant here: \(\sin\phi\left[\tfrac{\rho^3}{3}\right]_0^1 = \tfrac13\sin\phi\).

  3. \(\phi\) integral

    \(\tfrac13\big[-\cos\phi\big]_0^{\pi/2} = \tfrac13\).

  4. \(\theta\) integral

    \(\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}\frac13\,d\theta\). Multiply by the width of the interval.

Reveal the answer
\(\dfrac{2\pi}{3} \approx 2.094\)

A hemisphere of radius 1 has volume \(\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac43\pi\), so this matches geometry.

Topic 6

General spherical solids

The limits can be functions here too, following the same rule as before: only inner limits may depend on outer variables.

\[\iiint_E f\,dV = \int_\alpha^\beta\!\!\int_{g_1(\theta)}^{g_2(\theta)}\!\!\int_{u_1(\rho,\theta)}^{u_2(\rho,\theta)} f(\rho,\theta,\phi)\,\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\phi\,d\rho\,d\theta\]

How to translate conditions. Each inequality on \(x\), \(y\) or \(z\) becomes a limit on one spherical coordinate:

ConditionMeansSpherical limit
\(z \ge 0\)Above the equator\(0 \le \phi \le \tfrac{\pi}{2}\)
\(y \ge 0\)The half of space on the \(+y\) side\(0 \le \theta \le \pi\)
\(x \ge 0\)The half of space on the \(+x\) side\(-\tfrac{\pi}{2} \le \theta \le \tfrac{\pi}{2}\)
\(x^2+y^2+z^2 \le c^2\)Inside the sphere\(0 \le \rho \le c\)
Example 4Quarter ballnotes p. 10

Integrate \(f(x,y,z) = 5x^2 + 5y^2 + 5z^2\) over the solid region inside \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1\) with \(z \ge 0\) and \(y \ge 0\).

  1. Translate the region

    Inside the unit sphere: \(0 \le \rho \le 1\). Above the equator (\(z \ge 0\)): \(0 \le \phi \le \tfrac{\pi}{2}\). Halfway around (\(y \ge 0\)): \(0 \le \theta \le \pi\).

  2. Translate the function

    \(5(x^2 + y^2 + z^2) = 5\rho^2\). The value depends on distance only.

  3. Set it up with \(dV\)
    \[\int_0^{\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^1 5\rho^2\cdot\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\]
  4. \(\rho\) factor

    \(\int_0^1 5\rho^4\,d\rho = \big[\rho^5\big]_0^1 = 1\).

  5. \(\phi\) and \(\theta\) factors

    \(\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin\phi\,d\phi = 1\), and \(\int_0^{\pi} d\theta\) is the width of its interval. Multiply all three.

Reveal the answer
\(\pi\)\(1 \cdot 1 \cdot \pi\).

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting the \(r\) in \(dA\), or the \(\rho^2\sin\phi\) in \(dV\).
  • Treating the \(r\) in \(f\) as if it were the \(r\) from \(dA\). If the problem says \(dA\), add one. If it already says \(r\,dr\,d\theta\), don't.
  • Using a variable in an outer limit. Outer limits are always numbers.
  • Mixing up \(r\) and \(\rho\). \(r\) is the distance from the \(z\)-axis. \(\rho\) is the distance from the origin.
  • Mixing up \(\theta\) and \(\phi\). \(\phi\) is measured down from the positive \(z\)-axis and stops at \(\pi\).
  • Assuming \(z \ge 0\) limits \(\theta\). It limits \(\phi\). Conditions on \(x\) and \(y\) limit \(\theta\).
  • Forgetting to divide by the volume when finding an average value.
  • Using degrees in the limits. They must be radians.
