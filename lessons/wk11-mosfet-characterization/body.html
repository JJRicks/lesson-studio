MOSFETs: Current Controlled by Voltage

Every transistor so far has been a BJT, which needs a steady current into its base to stay on. This week's part, the MOSFET, is steered by voltage alone. Its control terminal, the gate, sits on a layer of glass, so no steady current flows into it at all. The homework measures two of them in LTspice: the n-channel 2N7002 and its p-channel opposite, the BSS84. For each one you'll find the gate voltage where it turns on, and draw the family of curves that shows how it behaves at every voltage.

Start here · no prior knowledge needed

The big picture: a switch you hold with voltage

A transistor lets a small signal control a big current. A microcontroller pin turns on a motor, a microphone's tiny voltage drives a speaker, and one logic gate flips the next. The BJTs from the last few weeks do this well, but they charge a fee. Their base needs a steady current the whole time they're on. With β = 100, holding 500 mA on costs 5 mA at the base, for as long as it stays on. Last week's regulator needed two transistors stacked into a Darlington just to shrink the op-amp's share to 68 µA.

A MOSFET drops the fee. Its gate is a metal plate resting on a very thin layer of glass, silicon dioxide, so current has no path in. You put a voltage on the gate, and the gate's electric field opens a path between the other two terminals. Once the gate is charged, holding the transistor on costs nothing. That's why a phone's processor can hold billions of them without melting, and why the switches inside phone chargers and laptop power supplies are MOSFETs.

Before you design with a transistor, you need to know its personality. Datasheets give ranges, because no two parts come out of the factory alike. This homework measures that personality in LTspice, with two pictures of each part:

  • Problem 1 sweeps the 2N7002's gate voltage to find its threshold, the voltage where it turns on.
  • Problem 2 draws the 2N7002's family of curves, which shows where it behaves like a switch and where it behaves like a current source.
  • Problem 3 finds the threshold of the BSS84, a p-channel MOSFET. It's the n-channel's mirror image, with every voltage and current flipped.
  • Problem 4 draws the BSS84's family, and the topic before it shows why circuits use both kinds together.
Analogy · two kinds of tap

A BJT is like the spring-loaded tap in a public restroom. Water runs only while you keep pressing, so holding it open costs you effort the whole time. A MOSFET is an ordinary kitchen tap. Turn the handle once and it stays where you left it. Here, effort means current. A BJT's base current never stops, while a MOSFET's gate takes a quick sip of charge when you change it, and then nothing.

A MOSFET is controlled by its gate voltage, and its gate draws no steady current.

Start here · from first principles

What a MOSFET is

What it is. MOSFET stands for metal–oxide–semiconductor field-effect transistor. The first three words name its layers from the top down: a metal gate, a thin oxide (glass) insulator, and the semiconductor, silicon. "Field effect" says how it works. The gate's electric field does the controlling, and no current is involved.

Why we care. It's a switch that stays off until you say otherwise, that you turn on with a voltage, and that costs nothing to hold on. That's the combination digital logic and power switching both need.

You'll wire up three terminals:

  • The source (S) is where the charge carriers come from.
  • The drain (D) is where they leave.
  • The gate (G) decides whether they can get from one to the other.

A fourth connection, the body, is the slab of silicon everything is built in. In parts like the 2N7002 it's tied to the source inside the package, so you never wire it. Here's how the n-channel kind works. Follow along in the demo at the top of the page.

  1. The body is p-type silicon. It has been doped so it's full of mobile positive charges called holes.
  2. The source and drain are two wells of n-type silicon, packed with free electrons. Between them lies p-type body, and an n–p–n stack like that blocks current in both directions. With 0 V on the gate, the part is off.
  3. Put a positive voltage on the gate. The gate and the silicon under it act like the two plates of a capacitor. The gate's + charge pushes holes away from the surface and pulls electrons toward it.
  4. Once the gate voltage passes the threshold, enough electrons gather at the surface to form a thin n-type sheet that joins source to drain. That sheet is the channel, and current can now flow along it.

The channel has to be created before anything conducts, so this kind is called enhancement mode. Both of this week's parts are enhancement-mode MOSFETs.

Analogy · a pontoon bridge

Two towns sit on opposite banks of a river with no road between them. Raising the gate voltage is like floating pontoons out onto the water. Below the threshold there aren't enough to reach across. Past it they join into a bridge, and traffic flows. More gate voltage packs in more pontoons and makes the bridge wider.

Reading the symbols
  • The gap between the gate line and the channel line is the oxide. No wire crosses it, which is why no DC current flows into the gate.
  • The channel line is broken into three pieces. That marks enhancement mode: no channel until the gate makes one.
  • The arrow sits on the body connection. It points in for n-channel and out for p-channel, the same way a diode's arrow points from p to n.
  • An n-channel part is drawn with its drain on top, toward the positive supply. A p-channel part is drawn with its source on top, because its source connects to the most positive voltage.

A positive gate pulls electrons into a sheet under the oxide, and that sheet is the channel the current flows through.

Go deeper: the gate does take current, for an instant

The gate is a capacitor, so changing its voltage moves charge: \(i = C\,dv/dt\). LTspice's 2N7002 model lists a total gate charge of 1.5 nC. Switch it in 10 ns and the gate takes a pulse of about 150 mA for those 10 ns, and then the current stops. Holding the gate at any steady voltage takes no current at all, and that's the difference from a BJT. Fast power circuits use a "gate driver" chip just to deliver those brief pulses quickly.

Problem 1 · the 2N7002's transfer curve · 5 pts

Where does it turn on?

The big idea

The threshold voltage tells you what can switch a MOSFET on. Tie the 2N7002's drain to its gate, sweep the gate voltage, and put a cursor where the current reaches 250 µA. The gate voltage at that point is its threshold.

Topic 1a · from first principles

The threshold voltage, VT

What it is. The threshold voltage \(V_T\) is the gate-to-source voltage where the channel has formed and the MOSFET starts to conduct. Below it the part is off. Above it the current climbs fast.

Why we care. It decides what can turn the part on. A microcontroller pin puts out 3.3 V, so a MOSFET with \(V_T\) near 1.6 V turns on hard from that pin. One with a \(V_T\) of 4 V would barely conduct, and the motor it was meant to drive wouldn't start. \(V_T\) also sets how much noise the gate can shrug off. A gate held at 0 V stays off even with a few hundred millivolts of noise on it, because the noise never reaches \(V_T\).

The turn-on isn't a sharp edge. Below the threshold a tiny current still leaks through. In LTspice's models it grows about 10 times for every 0.12 V, which makes it real but small enough to ignore in a switch. Since there's no single point where the part starts, manufacturers pick a current and call the gate voltage that produces it the threshold.

  • The 2N7002 datasheet uses 250 µA, and says \(V_T\) can land anywhere from 1 to 2.5 V.
  • The BSS84, like many discrete MOSFETs, uses 1 mA.

The range is wide because manufacturing varies. The oxide's thickness and the silicon's doping change a little from batch to batch, and \(V_T\) depends on both. That's why the homework has you measure it.

Above the threshold, with enough drain voltage (Topic 2a says how much), the current follows the square law. The amount \(V_{GS} - V_T\) is called the overdrive: how far past the threshold the gate is pushed.

The square law · saturation\[I_D = \frac{K_p}{2}\,\left(V_{GS} - V_T\right)^2\]

\(K_p\), in A/V², measures how strong the part is. Double the overdrive and the current goes up four times.

Analogy · a dam's spillway

Water behind a dam doesn't pour over until it rises to the spillway's lip. Below the lip only a little seeps through. Above it, the flow grows fast with every extra centimetre. The lip is \(V_T\), the seepage is the leakage below threshold, and the flow over the top is the square law.

Go deeper: the model's Vto isn't the datasheet's VT

LTspice's BSS123 model says Vto=1.6, yet the cursor finds 250 µA at about 1.46 V. The model's smooth turn-on (its ksubthres parameter) lets current start flowing before Vto, and Vto isn't tied to any particular current. So don't copy Vto out of a model file as your answer. Measure \(V_T\) the way the datasheet defines it.

\(V_T\) is the gate voltage at a stated drain current, which is 250 µA for the 2N7002.

Topic 1b · the test circuit

Why the drain is tied to the gate

What it is. Problem 1's circuit has a wire from the drain to the gate, so \(V_{DS} = V_{GS}\) at every point of the sweep. A MOSFET wired this way is called diode-connected, because it acts like a two-terminal part that starts conducting past a threshold voltage.

Why we care. It keeps the part in one region, saturation, where the square law holds. The datasheet measures \(V_T\) exactly this way, so your number means the same thing as theirs. And one source does the whole job.

Here's the reasoning. Topic 2a shows that a MOSFET is in saturation whenever \(V_{DS} \ge V_{GS} - V_T\). With the drain tied to the gate, \(V_{DS} = V_{GS}\), which is always bigger than \(V_{GS} - V_T\) by exactly \(V_T\). So whenever the part conducts, it's in saturation.

Problem 2 · the 2N7002's output curves · 4 pts

A switch and a current source in one part

The big idea

Hold the gate at one voltage and sweep the drain, and a MOSFET shows two personalities. At small \(V_{DS}\) it acts like a resistor, which is how it works as a switch. Past a knee the current goes flat, which is how it works in an amplifier. Problem 2 draws five of these curves in one run.

Topic 2a · from first principles

Cutoff, triode and saturation

What it is. Depending on its two voltages, a MOSFET is in one of three regions.

  • Cutoff, when \(V_{GS} \lt V_T\). There's no channel, so there's essentially no current.
  • Triode, when \(V_{GS} \gt V_T\) and \(V_{DS} \lt V_{GS} - V_T\). The channel runs from end to end, and the part acts like a resistor whose value the gate sets.
  • Saturation, when \(V_{GS} \gt V_T\) and \(V_{DS} \ge V_{GS} - V_T\). The channel is pinched off at the drain end, and the current levels out at the square-law value however high \(V_{DS}\) goes.

Why we care. Each region is a job. A switch runs in triode. You want lots of current with very little voltage left across the part, so it wastes little power, and a good MOSFET switch is a fraction of an ohm. An amplifier runs in saturation. There the current depends on \(V_{GS}\) alone, so the gate sets the current directly, whatever the load does to \(V_{DS}\).

Why does the current level out? The channel's thickness at each point depends on the voltage between the gate and that point. At the source end it's \(V_{GS}\). At the drain end it's only \(V_{GS} - V_{DS}\), because the drain sits higher. Raise \(V_{DS}\) and the channel thins toward the drain. When \(V_{GS} - V_{DS}\) falls to \(V_T\), the channel vanishes at the drain end. This is called pinch-off. Electrons still cross the tiny gap, flung across by the field, but the channel's shape stops changing, so the current stops growing. Try it in the demo at the top of the page: set \(V_{GS}\) to 3 V and slide \(V_{DS}\) up.

The three equations

Triode, when \(V_{DS} \lt V_{GS} - V_T\):

\[I_D = K_p\left[(V_{GS} - V_T)\,V_{DS} - \tfrac{1}{2}V_{DS}^2\right]\]

Saturation, when \(V_{DS} \ge V_{GS} - V_T\):

\[I_D = \frac{K_p}{2}\,(V_{GS} - V_T)^2\]

The edge between them:

\[V_{DS,sat} = V_{GS} - V_T\]

The edge depends on \(V_{GS}\), so the knee moves right as the gate voltage rises. Mark the knee of every possible curve and the marks line up along a parabola, the dashed line in the plot below.

Analogy · a waterfall

A river runs downhill to a waterfall. Lower the pool at the foot of the falls and the river above doesn't run any faster, because once the water goes over the edge, the river can't feel what's below. The pool's level is \(V_{DS}\). Before the edge forms (triode), lowering the pool speeds the river up. After it forms (saturation), the flow is set upstream, by the gate.

The family of curves · BSS123

Pick a curve, then drag along it. In the blue triode region the "acts like" tile shows a resistor, whose value grows as you move right. Watch out. "Saturation" means opposite things for the two kinds of transistor. A MOSFET in saturation is on the flat part of its curve, acting as a current source, which matches a BJT's active region. A BJT in saturation is switched fully on with almost no voltage across it, which matches a MOSFET's triode region.

Left of the knee a MOSFET is a resistor set by its gate. Right of the knee it's a current source set by its gate.

Problem 3 · the BSS84's transfer curve · 5 pts

The mirror image

The big idea

A p-channel MOSFET is the n-channel's mirror image. It turns on when its gate is pulled below its source, and its current flows the other way. Sweep \(V_{SG}\) in place of \(V_{GS}\) and Problem 1's method works unchanged, with the threshold coming out as a positive \(|V_T|\).

Topic 3a · from first principles

The p-channel MOSFET

What it is. Swap every n for a p. The body is n-type silicon, and the source and drain are p-type wells full of holes. Pull the gate below the source by more than \(|V_T|\), and the gate's negative charge draws holes up into a p-type channel. Current then flows in at the source, along the channel, and out of the drain.

Why we care. An n-channel switch has to sit between its load and ground, because its gate must rise above its source. Many loads need their connection to the positive supply switched instead, like a phone cutting power to a whole section of its circuitry. A p-channel part does that job. Its source sits on the supply, and you turn it on by pulling its gate low. Pairing one of each also makes CMOS logic, the subject of Problem 4's topic.

Every sign flips, which is why the homework renames the voltages.

Analogy · left-handed scissors

Left-handed scissors do exactly what right-handed ones do, with every part mirrored, and they work the same as long as you hold them the mirrored way. A p-channel MOSFET is the left-handed version: the same behaviour, with every voltage and current reversed.

The same test, built both ways

Flip between the two. The n-channel circuit lifts the tied gate and drain above a grounded source. The p-channel one, drawn the way Problem 3 has it, puts the source on top at ground and pulls the gate and drain below it. Every reading keeps its size and flips its sign. The p-channel part here is a made-up mirror image of the BSS123, so the two compare like for like. A real p-channel part of the same size is weaker, because holes move two to three times slower than electrons.

Problem 4 · the BSS84's output curves · 4 pts

The family, upside down

The big idea

The BSS84's family has the same three regions as the 2N7002's. With \(V_{SG}\) and \(V_{SD}\) in place of \(V_{GS}\) and \(V_{DS}\) everything reads the same, except that LTspice draws the currents negative. Pair the two kinds and you get CMOS, the circuit inside nearly every digital chip.

Topic 4a · why we care

Why we need both kinds: CMOS

What it is. CMOS, short for complementary MOS, stacks a p-channel and an n-channel transistor between the supply and ground, with their gates tied together. The simplest CMOS circuit is the inverter. The p-channel part runs from the supply down to the output, and the n-channel part runs from the output down to ground.

  • With the input high, the n-channel part turns on and the p-channel part turns off. The output is pulled down to ground.
  • With the input low, the p-channel part turns on and the n-channel part turns off. The output is pulled up to the supply.

Why we care. In either settled state, one of the two is off, so there's no path from the supply to ground. A CMOS gate that isn't switching draws almost no current. It spends power only in the brief moment of switching, when both transistors are partly on. That's how a phone's processor holds billions of transistors and still runs all day on a battery.

A CMOS inverter · live

Slide Vin from 0 to 5 V. Near either end the output sits at a clean 5 V or 0 V, and the supply current is practically nothing. Only near the middle, where both transistors are partly on, does current flow straight through from top to bottom. The demo uses a BSS123 and its mirror image, so it switches exactly halfway.

Problem 4 doesn't build an inverter. It characterizes the BSS84 the same way Problem 2 did the 2N7002, which is the first step toward designing one.

An n-channel and a p-channel MOSFET in series make CMOS logic, which draws current only while it switches.

Cheat sheet

Threshold

2N7002: \(V_{GS}\) where \(I_D\) = 250 µA.

BSS84: \(V_{SG}\) where \(I_D\) = −1 mA. That's \(|V_T|\).

Regions
cutoff: \(V_{GS} \lt V_T\)
triode: \(V_{DS} \lt V_{GS} - V_T\)
saturation: \(V_{DS} \ge V_{GS} - V_T\)
Square law
\(I_D = \tfrac{K_p}{2}(V_{GS} - V_T)^2\)

In saturation. Double the overdrive and the current quadruples.

Triode
\(I_D = K_p\left[(V_{GS} - V_T)V_{DS} - \tfrac{1}{2}V_{DS}^2\right]\)

A resistor whose value the gate sets.

Drain tied to gate

\(V_{DS} = V_{GS}\), so the part is in saturation whenever it conducts.

p-channel signs

Use \(V_{SG}\) and \(V_{SD}\), both positive when it's on. \(V_T\) is negative, and LTspice's Id(M1) is negative.

Two-source .dc
.dc V1 a b step V2 c d step

V1 is the x-axis. Each V2 value draws one curve.

LTspice moves

Click the drain for Id(M1). Click the trace's name for a cursor. Click the axis numbers to set the range.

Gate current

Zero at any steady gate voltage. A brief pulse only while the gate voltage changes.

Common mistakes

  • Copying Vto (1.6 V, or −2.1 V for the BSS84) out of the model as the answer. Measure \(V_T\) at the datasheet's current.
  • Reporting the cursor's Vert value. The answer is Horz, the voltage where the current reaches its target.
  • Hunting for exactly 250 µA. The sweep's points are 1 mV apart, so take the closest one.
  • Leaving the axis at full scale, where 250 µA is too small to see.
  • Reading a MOSFET's "saturation" as fully on, the way it is for a BJT. A fully-on MOSFET is in triode.
  • Putting the sources in the wrong order in a two-source sweep. The first one is the x-axis.
  • Expecting a positive Id(M1) from the BSS84. LTspice counts current into the drain, and here it flows out.
  • Dropping the sign. The BSS84's sweep reads \(|V_T|\) = \(V_{SG}\), and \(V_T\) itself is negative.
