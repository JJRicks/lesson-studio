The Common-Emitter Amplifier

A few millivolts go in, a few volts come out, and the wave comes out upside down. One transistor, four resistors and three capacitors make that happen. This page builds every idea from scratch, then works all three problems step by step, with each final answer hidden until you click.

Start here · no prior knowledge needed

Two signals share one wire

Electronics uses two kinds of voltage, and an amplifier needs both at once.

  • DC (direct current) stays put. A 15 V supply is DC.
  • AC (alternating current) wiggles up and down. A microphone's output is AC. How fast it wiggles is its frequency \(f\), measured in hertz (Hz), which means cycles per second.

A transistor can't amplify a wiggle by itself. It has to be switched partly on first, with a steady DC current already flowing. Setting up that steady current is called biasing. The AC signal then rides on top and nudges the current up and down.

Analogy · a boat on a lake

DC is the water level, and AC is the ripples on the surface. The ripples go up and down around whatever level the lake is at. An amplifier sets a good water level first, so the ripples have room to grow in both directions.

Capacitors are what let these two share a circuit. They let the AC wiggles through while keeping the DC levels on each side separate. That job is the subject of Problems 1 and 2. Problem 3 then finds the DC levels and the AC gain.

DC sets the stage. AC is the performance. Capacitors keep the two apart.

Problem 1 · 4 pts

Coupling capacitors

The big idea

A coupling capacitor passes the AC signal and blocks DC. It only passes the signal well when its opposition to AC is small next to the resistance it feeds. Since that opposition grows at low frequency, good coupling has a minimum frequency.

Topic 1 · from first principles

What a capacitor does

A capacitor is two metal plates with an insulator between them. Current can't cross the gap. Charge can still pile up on one plate and drain off the other, and that takes current in the wires on both sides.

The more charge \(Q\) that piles up, the more voltage \(V\) builds across the gap. The ratio is its capacitance \(C\), measured in farads. Real parts are usually microfarads (µF, millionths of a farad).

\[Q = C V\]
  • DC. Connect a battery. Current flows until the capacitor's voltage matches the battery, then stops for good. To DC, a capacitor is an open circuit, a break in the path.
  • AC. The voltage keeps reversing, so charge flows onto the plate, back off, and onto it again. The current never gets to stop. To AC, a capacitor acts like a connection, and a better one as the frequency rises.
Analogy · a rubber sheet across a pipe

Stretch a rubber sheet across a water pipe. Push steadily and it bulges, then stops: no steady flow can get past it. Push back and forth and the water on the far side moves back and forth as well, even though no water crosses the sheet. Fast small pushes barely stretch it at all. A bigger sheet stretches less for the same push.

A capacitor and a resistor · live
sourceacross the resistor (what gets through)across the capacitor (what's lost)

The resistor is 1 kΩ. Raise the frequency and the resistor's trace grows to match the source while the capacitor's trace shrinks. Pick DC and watch the current die away as the capacitor fills. The waves are slowed down so each cycle takes the same time on screen.

DC can't get through a capacitor. AC can, and it gets through more easily at higher frequency and with more capacitance.

Topic 2 · a number for "how much it resists"

Reactance

A resistor's opposition to current is its resistance. A capacitor's opposition to AC is called its reactance, written \(X_C\). It's also measured in ohms. Here's where the formula comes from.

  1. Current is the rate charge moves

    Since \(Q = CV\), current is \(i = C\,\dfrac{dv}{dt}\). The faster the voltage changes, the more current flows.

  2. Use a sine wave

    Let \(v = V_p \sin(2\pi f t)\). Taking the rate of change gives \(i = 2\pi f C\,V_p \cos(2\pi f t)\).

  3. Compare the peaks

    The current's peak is \(I_p = 2\pi f C\,V_p\). Dividing voltage by current, the same way you'd find a resistance, gives the reactance.

Capacitive reactance\[X_C = \frac{1}{2\pi f C}\]

Both \(f\) and \(C\) sit on the bottom, so raising either one lowers \(X_C\).

  • Double the frequency and \(X_C\) halves. The charge has half the time to pile up, so less voltage builds.
  • Double the capacitance and \(X_C\) halves. The plates hold twice the charge for the same voltage.
Quick check: what is \(X_C\) at DC, where \(f = 0\)?

Infinite, because you'd be dividing by zero. That's the formula saying a capacitor blocks DC.

Topic 3 · what "good coupling" means

The 10× rule

A coupling capacitor sits in series with whatever it feeds, which means the same current passes through one and then the other. The resistance it feeds is called \(R_{eq}\), the equivalent resistance. Together they form a voltage divider, which shares the input voltage between them. The part of the signal that lands on \(R_{eq}\) is the part that got through.

Reactance and resistance don't add like two resistors, because a capacitor's voltage is a quarter cycle out of step with its current. They combine like the two sides of a right triangle:

\[\frac{v_{out}}{v_{in}} = \frac{R_{eq}}{\sqrt{R_{eq}^2 + X_C^2}}\]
\(X_C\) compared with \(R_{eq}\)Signal that gets through
\(X_C = 10\,R_{eq}\)10%
\(X_C = R_{eq}\)71%
\(X_C = 0.1\,R_{eq}\)99.5%

The last row is the design rule. Once \(X_C\) is a tenth of \(R_{eq}\), the capacitor is close enough to a plain wire.

Good coupling\[X_C \lt 0.1\,R_{eq}\]

\(X_C\) shrinks as \(f\) rises, so the rule holds at high frequencies and fails at low ones. Setting \(X_C\) equal to \(0.1\,R_{eq}\) finds the edge, the lowest frequency where coupling is still good:

\[f_{min} = \frac{1}{2\pi C\,(0.1\,R_{eq})}\]

Good coupling means \(X_C\) is under a tenth of what the capacitor feeds. Find where they meet, and everything above that frequency is fine.

Topic 4 · finding Req

What the capacitor sees

To find \(R_{eq}\), stand at the capacitor and ask what resistance the AC signal runs into. Three facts settle it.

  • An ideal AC source has no resistance of its own. The problem shows a plain source, so nothing adds on that side.
  • A DC supply can't wiggle. Its voltage is held constant. For AC, any point that doesn't wiggle is the same as ground. So \(V_{cc}\) is an AC ground.
  • That makes R1 and R2 parallel. R1 used to run from the signal's node (a point where parts connect) up to \(V_{cc}\). For AC, it runs from that node to ground, exactly like R2.
\[R_{eq} = R_1 \parallel R_2 = \frac{R_1 R_2}{R_1 + R_2}\]

The symbol \(\parallel\) means "in parallel with". Two parallel paths always pass current more easily than either alone, so the result is smaller than the smaller resistor.

Analogy · two drains in one sink

Water leaves a sink faster through two drains than through either one. It doesn't matter that one pipe runs up to the roof tank and the other down to the sewer. If the roof tank's level never changes, a ripple sees both as places to go.

Watch the circuit redraw itself

These are example values. Make R1 much larger than R2 and see \(R_{eq}\) settle just under R2. The smaller resistor does most of the deciding.

For AC, \(V_{cc}\) is ground. Anything that connected to \(V_{cc}\) now connects to ground.

Quick check: what is 10 kΩ in parallel with 10 kΩ?

5 kΩ. Two equal resistors in parallel give half of one.

Problem 2 · 4 pts

Bypass capacitors

The big idea

The emitter resistor keeps the DC current stable, but it would throw away most of the AC gain. A capacitor across it gives AC a shortcut to ground while DC still has to go through the resistor. It uses the same 10× rule, solved for \(C\) this time.

Topic 1 · the second capacitor job

Why bypass the emitter resistor

R4 sits between the emitter and ground. It's there for a good reason, which Problem 3 explains: it makes the DC current stable. For AC, though, it causes trouble.

When the signal pushes more current through the transistor, that current also flows through R4. R4's voltage rises, which lifts the emitter. The input is trying to raise the base relative to the emitter, and the rising emitter cancels most of that push. The gain collapses.

A bypass capacitor across R4 fixes this. DC can't pass through a capacitor, so the DC current still has to go through R4 and stays stable. AC takes the capacitor, which holds the emitter nearly still.

R4 is a speed bump that keeps the steady traffic calm. The bypass capacitor is a side lane that only opens for traffic that keeps changing direction. Commuters going back and forth skip the bump, and the steady flow still has to cross it.

Going further: what the 10× rule leaves out

Look at the chart above. Where the dashed line sits, the gain is well short of its top. The rule compares \(X_C\) with R4, and that keeps C3 small enough to be practical, but C3 also has to beat a much smaller resistance looking back into the emitter. That resistance is \(r_e'\), which you'll meet in Problem 3.

To get the full gain all the way down to the lowest frequency, a real design would compare \(X_C\) against \(R_4 \parallel r_e'\), which needs a far larger capacitor. Problem 2 only gives R4, so R4 is what you use here. If you simulate an amplifier and the low-frequency gain sags, this is why.

C3 hides R4 from the AC signal and leaves it in place for DC.

Topic 2 · solving for C

Choosing the capacitor

Problem 1 knew \(C\) and solved for \(f\). A design problem goes the other way. Rearrange the same formula for \(C\):

\[C = \frac{1}{2\pi f\,X_C} \qquad\text{with}\quad X_C = 0.1\,R_{eq}\]

Two decisions come with it.

  • Which frequency? Use the lowest one the circuit has to handle. \(X_C\) is largest there, so if the rule holds at the bottom it holds everywhere above.
  • Which value? Capacitors are only sold in standard values. The 10% series has twelve steps per decade (one factor of ten): 10, 12, 15, 18, 22, 27, 33, 39, 47, 56, 68 and 82. Each repeats at every power of ten, so 18 nF, 18 µF and 180 µF all exist.

Always round up. A bigger capacitor means a smaller \(X_C\), which only makes the rule more true. Rounding down breaks it.

The 10% capacitor ladder
Quick check: you calculate 25 µF. Which standard value do you buy?

27 µF. The next step up from 25 on the list is 27. Picking 22 µF would leave \(X_C\) above the limit.

Problem 3 · 10 pts · five parts, 2 pts each

The voltage divider bias amplifier

The big idea

Solve the circuit twice. The DC pass finds the steady current through the transistor. The AC pass finds the gain, and that steady current sets the value of the transistor's tiny internal resistance, which decides how big the gain is.

Topic 1 · the three legs

The transistor, briefly

A bipolar junction transistor (BJT) has three legs: the base, the collector and the emitter. A small current into the base controls a much larger current from collector to emitter. Three facts cover this homework.

  • The base-emitter path is a diode, a one-way valve for current. When it's on (forward-biased), the base sits about 0.7 V above the emitter.
  • The collector current is the base current times the current gain β: \(I_C = \beta I_B\). Here β is 300.
  • The emitter carries both: \(I_E = I_C + I_B\). With β this large, \(I_B\) is tiny, so \(I_C \approx I_E\).

An amplifier keeps the transistor in its active region, partly on, where the collector current can rise and fall with the input. Fully on or fully off would clip the wave, flattening its peaks.

Analogy · a valve held partly open

Think of a big water pipe with a valve. A hand barely moving the valve changes a lot of water flow. For this to work in both directions, the valve has to start partly open. Biasing is what holds it there.

Topic 3 · finding VB

Voltage divider bias

R1 and R2 form a voltage divider: two resistors in series across the supply, with the base tapped from the middle. Ignoring base current for the moment, the same current flows through both, \(I = V_{cc}/(R_1+R_2)\). The voltage at the middle is that current times R2.

Voltage divider\[V_B = V_{cc}\,\frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}\]

The formula only holds if the base draws very little of that current. A divider that isn't disturbed by the base is called stiff. The usual test compares R2 with the resistance the emitter reflects back to the base, which is about \(\beta R_E\).

Stiff divider test\[R_2 \le 0.01\,\beta\,R_E\]

In this homework β only appears in this check. Once the divider passes, the simplified formulas don't use it again.

Analogy · sipping from a fast stream

Dip a cup in a fast stream and the water level doesn't change. Dip it in a trickle and you've taken most of what was there. The divider's current is the stream, and the base current is the cup.

A stiff divider acts like a fixed voltage source. The base takes a sip and the level doesn't change.

Topic 4 · finding IE

The emitter current

Once you know the base voltage, the emitter takes care of itself. The base-emitter diode holds the emitter one diode drop below the base. Then Ohm's law, \(I = V/R\), gives the current through the emitter resistor.

DC emitter current\[V_E = V_B - 0.7\text{ V} \qquad I_E = \frac{V_E}{R_E}\]

Notice what's missing. β isn't in either formula. That's the reason this circuit is popular. Transistors of the same part number can have β values that differ by a factor of three, and they also drift with temperature. This circuit sets its current with resistors, so it barely notices.

Compare it with the cruder base bias, which feeds the base through one resistor. That fixes \(I_B\), so \(I_C = \beta I_B\) follows β directly.

Topic 6 · finding re′

AC emitter resistance

The base-emitter junction is a diode, and a diode's current grows exponentially with its voltage:

\[I = I_S\, e^{V/V_T}\]

\(V_T\) is the thermal voltage, about 25 mV near room temperature. \(I_S\) is a tiny constant that depends on the transistor.

A signal only moves the junction a tiny distance along this curve. Over such a short stretch, the curve looks like a straight line, and a straight line on a current-voltage graph is a resistor. So the junction behaves like a small resistor for AC, called \(r_e'\). The prime is part of the name.

Its value is the inverse slope of the curve. Taking the derivative gives \(dI/dV = I/V_T\), and flipping that over gives the rule:

AC emitter resistance\[r_e' = \frac{25\text{ mV}}{I_E}\]

More DC current puts you higher up the curve where it's steeper, so \(r_e'\) is smaller. This is the link between the two halves of the analysis: the DC current sets an AC resistance.

Some books use 26 mV. This homework needs 25 mV, because the hint's range for the gain only works with it.

The diode curve, up close · live

Drag the current up. The dot climbs to where the curve is steeper, so the same small voltage wiggle makes a much bigger current wiggle. The dashed line is the tangent, and its steepness is \(1/r_e'\).

\(r_e'\) is the slope of the diode curve at the DC current. More DC current means less \(r_e'\).

Topic 7 · finding AV

Voltage gain

Voltage gain \(A_V\) is how many times bigger the output wave is than the input wave. Lowercase \(v\) and \(i\) mean the AC part. Follow the signal through the AC circuit.

  1. The input reaches the base

    C1 is a short for AC, so \(v_b = v_{in}\).

  2. All of it lands on \(r_e'\)

    C3 holds the emitter at AC ground. So the whole signal sits across the junction's small resistance, and Ohm's law gives \(i_e = v_{in}/r_e'\).

  3. The current flows into the collector

    Since \(i_c \approx i_e\), that same current runs through \(r_c\) and makes a voltage \(i_e r_c\).

  4. The output is upside down

    When the base rises, more current flows. More current means a bigger drop across \(r_c\), which pulls the collector down. So \(v_{out} = -i_e r_c\).

Divide the output by the input and \(i_e\) cancels:

Gain of a bypassed common-emitter amplifier\[A_V = \frac{v_{out}}{v_{in}} = -\frac{r_c}{r_e'}\]

The gain is the ratio of two resistances. The same current passes through a small one and a large one, and the voltage across the large one is proportionally bigger.

Without C3, R4 stays in the emitter's AC path, and the gain falls to \(-r_c/(r_e' + R_E)\).

Analogy · a see-saw with a long arm

Push the short end of a see-saw down a little and the long end swings up a lot. The input pushes on the short arm \(r_e'\), and the output is the long arm \(r_c\). The lengths set how much it multiplies, and the pivot is why the output goes the opposite way.

The gain see-saw · live

The arms are compressed to fit the screen, so read the true ratio from the numbers. Raising \(I_E\) shortens \(r_e'\) and raises the gain. Remove C3 and 560 Ω joins the short arm, so the see-saw barely multiplies at all.

Going further: why a simulator reads a little lower

The simplified formula leaves a few things out. The transistor has its own output resistance in parallel with \(r_c\), and at 27 °C the thermal voltage is 25.9 mV. Both pull the gain down by several percent. If you simulate this homework's circuit, expect a magnitude somewhat under the hand answer.

Gain is the collector's AC resistance over the emitter's AC resistance, with a minus sign for the flip.

Cheat sheet

Reactance
\(X_C = \dfrac{1}{2\pi f C}\)

Raising \(f\) or \(C\) lowers \(X_C\). At DC it's infinite.

Good coupling or bypassing
\(X_C \lt 0.1\,R_{eq}\)
\(f_{min} = \dfrac{1}{2\pi C(0.1 R_{eq})}\)
\(C_{min} = \dfrac{1}{2\pi f(0.1 R_{eq})}\)

Design at the lowest frequency. Round capacitors up.

DC or AC circuit
DC: caps open, \(V_{cc}\) on
AC: caps short, \(V_{cc}\) = ground

Parts tied to \(V_{cc}\) are tied to ground for AC.

DC analysis
\(V_B = V_{cc}\dfrac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}\)
\(V_E = V_B - 0.7\)
\(I_E = V_E/R_E\)

Stiff if \(R_2 \le 0.01\,\beta R_E\).

AC resistances
\(r_c = R_C \parallel R_L\)
\(r_e' = 25\text{ mV}/I_E\)

Parallel: \(R_1R_2/(R_1+R_2)\).

Gain
\(A_V = -\dfrac{r_c}{r_e'}\)
no \(C_3\): \(A_V = -\dfrac{r_c}{r_e' + R_E}\)

The minus sign means the output is inverted.

Common mistakes

  • Adding R1 and R2 in series for \(R_{eq}\). For AC they're in parallel, because \(V_{cc}\) is ground.
  • Forgetting the 0.1, and setting \(X_C\) equal to \(R_{eq}\).
  • Leaving capacitance in µF inside the formula. Convert to farads: 47 µF is \(47\times10^{-6}\) F.
  • Designing a capacitor at the highest frequency. The lowest one is the hard case.
  • Rounding a capacitor down to the nearest standard value. Always round up.
  • Forgetting to subtract 0.7 V, so that \(I_E\) comes out as \(V_B/R_E\).
  • Using R3 alone for \(r_c\) and leaving out the load.
  • Using \(I_B\) in \(r_e'\). The formula uses the emitter current.
  • Putting R4 in the gain formula when C3 is present. It's shorted for AC.
  • Dropping the minus sign on \(A_V\). It's part of the answer.
