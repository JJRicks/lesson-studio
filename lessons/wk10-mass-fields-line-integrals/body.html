Lesson 28 · balance and spin

Centers of mass and moments of inertia

The big idea

A plate whose weight is spread unevenly balances at one special point, called its center of mass. Three integrals find it: one for the total mass and two "moments" that measure how far the mass sits from each axis. Square those distances and you get the moment of inertia, which measures how hard the plate is to spin.

Topic 1 · from first principles

Mass from density

A lamina is a thin, flat plate, thin enough that you can ignore its thickness. Think of a sheet of metal or a cookie. It sits in the \(xy\)-plane and covers a region \(R\).

A plate can be heavier in some places than others. Its density \(\rho(x,y)\) (the Greek letter rho) says how much mass sits on each unit of area at the point \((x,y)\). It is measured in units like grams per square centimeter. If the density is the same everywhere, the plate is homogeneous.

Analogy · a pizza with uneven toppings

A pizza with all the cheese piled on one side weighs the same per square inch on the crust side as plain dough, and much more on the cheesy side. Density is "weight per square inch" at each spot. To weigh the whole pizza, you'd weigh every small square and add them up.

That is exactly the recipe for an integral:

  1. Chop

    Cut \(R\) into small rectangles, each with area \(\Delta A\).

  2. Weigh one piece

    A small piece is almost evenly dense, so its mass is about density × area, \(\rho(x^*,y^*)\,\Delta A\), using any point \((x^*,y^*)\) inside it.

  3. Add and refine

    Add up every piece and let the pieces shrink. The sum becomes a double integral.

Mass of a lamina \[m = \lim_{k,l\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{l}\rho(x^*_{ij},y^*_{ij})\,\Delta A = \iint_R \rho(x,y)\,dA\]

Here's a picture that ties this to last week. Draw the surface \(z = \rho(x,y)\) above the plate. Its height is the density, so the mass is the volume under that surface. Every tool you have for volumes works on mass.

Mass is the double integral of density. If the density were a constant, it would be density × area.

Example 1Mass of a laminanotes p. 4

Find the mass of the lamina on the rectangle with vertices \((0,0)\), \((2,0)\), \((2,3)\) and \((0,3)\), with density \(\rho(x,y) = 3y\sqrt{x}\). Give an exact answer.

  1. Read the plate

    The corners give \(0 \le x \le 2\) and \(0 \le y \le 3\). The density grows to the right (the \(\sqrt{x}\)) and toward the top (the \(3y\)).

  2. Set up the mass integral
    \[m = \int_0^2\!\!\int_0^3 3y\sqrt{x}\,dy\,dx\]
  3. Split it

    The density is an \(x\) piece times a \(y\) piece, and every limit is a number. So the double integral splits into two single integrals:

    \[m = \left(\int_0^2 \sqrt{x}\,dx\right)\left(\int_0^3 3y\,dy\right)\]
  4. The \(x\) part
    \[\int_0^2 x^{1/2}\,dx = \frac{2}{3}x^{3/2}\Big|_0^2 = \frac23\cdot 2\sqrt2 = \frac{4\sqrt2}{3}\]
  5. The \(y\) part, then multiply
    \[\int_0^3 3y\,dy = \frac32 y^2\Big|_0^3 = \frac{27}{2}\]

    Multiply the two parts.

Reveal the answer
\(m = 18\sqrt2 \approx 25.46\)\(\tfrac{4\sqrt2}{3}\cdot\tfrac{27}{2}\). The \(\tfrac{27}{3}\) reduces to 9 and the \(\tfrac{4}{2}\) to 2.

Sanity check: the densest corner is \((2,3)\), where \(\rho \approx 12.7\). If the whole plate (area 6) had that density, the mass would be about 76. The plate is much lighter near the axes, so 25 is reasonable.

Topic 2 · turning effect

Moments: mass times distance

Before you can find a balance point, you need a way to measure how strongly a weight tries to tip something.

Analogy · a seesaw

A small child far from the middle of a seesaw can balance a large adult sitting close in. What matters is weight × distance from the pivot. Physicists call that product a moment.

For a plate, each small piece has mass \(\rho\,\Delta A\). Its moment about a line is that mass times its distance from the line. Add up all the pieces:

  • The moment about the \(y\)-axis, \(M_y\), uses the distance from the \(y\)-axis. A point \((x,y)\) is \(x\) units from the \(y\)-axis.
  • The moment about the \(x\)-axis, \(M_x\), uses the distance from the \(x\)-axis, which is \(y\).
Moments of a lamina \[M_x = \iint_R y\,\rho(x,y)\,dA \qquad\qquad M_y = \iint_R x\,\rho(x,y)\,dA\]

The letters look swapped, and that trips everyone up. \(M_x\) has a \(y\) inside, and \(M_y\) has an \(x\) inside. The subscript names the axis you measure from. The distance to the \(x\)-axis is \(y\).

A distance to the left of the \(y\)-axis is negative \(x\), so mass on opposite sides cancels. That cancelling is what balancing means.

Example 2Moment about the \(y\)-axisnotes p. 4

Find the moment \(M_y\) about the \(y\)-axis for the lamina on the rectangle with vertices \((3,4)\), \((3,5)\), \((4,5)\) and \((4,4)\), with density \(\rho(x,y) = y\). Give an exact answer.

  1. Read the plate

    The corners give \(3 \le x \le 4\) and \(4 \le y \le 5\). The plate is a 1-by-1 square sitting well away from both axes.

  2. Pick the right distance

    \(M_y\) measures from the \(y\)-axis, so each piece's lever arm is its \(x\):

    \[M_y = \iint_R x\,\rho\,dA = \int_3^4\!\!\int_4^5 x\,y\,dy\,dx\]
  3. Split it

    The integrand \(xy\) is an \(x\) piece times a \(y\) piece over a rectangle:

    \[M_y = \left(\int_3^4 x\,dx\right)\left(\int_4^5 y\,dy\right)\]
  4. Each part, then multiply
    \[\int_3^4 x\,dx = \frac{16-9}{2} = \frac72 \qquad \int_4^5 y\,dy = \frac{25-16}{2} = \frac92\]

    Multiply the two parts.

Reveal the answer
\(M_y = \dfrac{63}{4} = 15.75\)\(\tfrac72\cdot\tfrac92\).

Sanity check: the plate's mass is \(\int_3^4\!\int_4^5 y\,dy\,dx = 4.5\), and every piece is between 3 and 4 units from the \(y\)-axis. So \(M_y\) should land between \(3(4.5) = 13.5\) and \(4(4.5) = 18\). It does.

Topic 3 · the balance point

Center of mass

The center of mass \((\bar x, \bar y)\) is the point where you could balance the plate on a pin. (Read \(\bar x\) as "x bar.") In a uniform gravity field it's the same as the center of gravity.

Here's where the formula comes from. Imagine squeezing all of the mass \(m\) into one point at \(\bar x\). Its moment about the \(y\)-axis would be \(m\,\bar x\). The real plate must tip the same way, so set the two equal: \(m\,\bar x = M_y\). Do the same for \(y\).

Mass, moments and center of mass \[m = \iint_R \rho\,dA \qquad M_x = \iint_R y\rho\,dA \qquad M_y = \iint_R x\rho\,dA\] \[\bar x = \frac{M_y}{m} \qquad\qquad \bar y = \frac{M_x}{m}\]

Another way to read \(\bar x\): it's the average \(x\) of the plate, where heavier spots count more. If the plate is homogeneous, every spot counts equally and the center of mass is the plain geometric center, called the centroid.

Balance a plate on a pin

The plate covers \(0 \le x \le 4\), \(0 \le y \le 3\), with density \(\rho = (1 + a\,x/4)(1 + b\,y/3)\). Each speck is a grain of mass, so crowded areas are denser. Drag the pin. The red arrow points the way the plate would tip, and it shrinks to nothing at the balance point. The green markers on the edges show \(\bar x\) and \(\bar y\). Watch \(\bar x = M_y/m\) slide right as you add weight on the right.

Example 3Center of massnotes p. 5

Find the center of mass \((\bar x, \bar y)\) of the lamina on the rectangle with vertices \((0,0)\), \((3,0)\), \((3,4)\) and \((0,4)\), with density \(\rho(x,y) = xy^3\). Give an exact answer.

  1. Read the plate and plan

    \(0 \le x \le 3\) and \(0 \le y \le 4\). You need three integrals: \(m\), \(M_y\) and \(M_x\). Each one splits into an \(x\) part times a \(y\) part.

  2. Mass
    \[m = \int_0^3 x\,dx\cdot\int_0^4 y^3\,dy = \frac92\cdot 64 = 288\]
  3. \(M_y\): multiply the density by \(x\)
    \[M_y = \int_0^3 x^2\,dx\cdot\int_0^4 y^3\,dy = 9\cdot 64 = 576\]
  4. \(M_x\): multiply the density by \(y\)
    \[M_x = \int_0^3 x\,dx\cdot\int_0^4 y^4\,dy = \frac92\cdot\frac{1024}{5} = \frac{4608}{5}\]
  5. Divide

    \(\bar x = M_y/m\) and \(\bar y = M_x/m\). Compute them.

Reveal the answer
\((\bar x, \bar y) = \left(2,\ \dfrac{16}{5}\right) = (2,\ 3.2)\)\(576/288 = 2\) and \(\tfrac{4608}{5}\div 288 = \tfrac{16}{5}\).

Sanity check: the plain center of the rectangle is \((1.5, 2)\). The density grows with \(x\) and very fast with \(y\) (that's the \(y^3\)), so the balance point moves right a little and up a lot. It does.

Topic 4 · resistance to spinning

Moments of inertia

Mass measures how hard it is to push something into a straight-line motion. The moment of inertia \(I\) measures how hard it is to start something spinning about an axis, or to stop it once it spins.

For one small mass \(m\) at distance \(r\) from the axis, \(I = m r^2\). The distance is squared, so mass far from the axis counts a lot. Twice as far means four times the resistance.

Analogy · a figure skater

A skater spinning with arms out turns slowly. When they pull their arms in, the same mass moves closer to the axis, the moment of inertia drops, and they spin much faster. A broom is also easy to twirl around its handle and hard to swing around its far end.

Twirl test · same push, different spread

Each rod holds two 1 kg weights. Both get the same twisting push for 3 seconds, then the test restarts. The spin they reach is the push divided by \(I = 2\cdot 1\cdot r^2\). Set one distance to double the other and the near rod spins four times as fast.

For a plate, add up \(r^2\) times the mass of each small piece. The distance \(r\) depends on the axis you spin about:

  • About the \(x\)-axis, a point \((x,y)\) is \(y\) units away, so \(r^2 = y^2\).
  • About the \(y\)-axis, it's \(x\) units away, so \(r^2 = x^2\).
  • About the origin (spinning flat in the plane, like a record), it's \(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) away, so \(r^2 = x^2+y^2\). This one is the polar moment of inertia.
Moments of inertia of a lamina \[I_x = \iint_R y^2\rho\,dA \qquad I_y = \iint_R x^2\rho\,dA \qquad I_0 = I_x + I_y = \iint_R (x^2+y^2)\rho\,dA\]

Same swap as before: \(I_x\) has \(y^2\) inside. The subscript names the spin axis, and you measure distance to that axis.

Example 4Moment of inertianotes p. 7

Find the moment of inertia \(I_x\) about the \(x\)-axis for the lamina on the square with vertices \((0,0)\), \((0,2)\), \((2,2)\) and \((2,0)\), with density \(\rho(x,y) = x^2y^2\). Give an exact answer.

  1. The distance to the axis

    The plate spins about the \(x\)-axis. A point \((x,y)\) is \(y\) units from that axis, so each piece contributes \(y^2\) times its mass:

    \[I_x = \iint_R y^2\,\rho\,dA\]
  2. Set it up
    \[I_x = \int_0^2\!\!\int_0^2 y^2\cdot x^2y^2\,dy\,dx = \int_0^2\!\!\int_0^2 x^2y^4\,dy\,dx\]
  3. Split it
    \[I_x = \left(\int_0^2 x^2\,dx\right)\left(\int_0^2 y^4\,dy\right)\]
  4. Each part, then multiply
    \[\int_0^2 x^2\,dx = \frac83 \qquad \int_0^2 y^4\,dy = \frac{32}{5}\]

    Multiply the two parts.

Reveal the answer
\(I_x = \dfrac{256}{15} \approx 17.07\)\(\tfrac83\cdot\tfrac{32}{5}\).

The units are mass × length². The \(y^4\) shows that the far edge at \(y = 2\) does almost all the resisting.

Quick check (notes Practice 1): \(I_y\) for the rectangle \(0 \le x \le 6,\ 0 \le y \le 2\) with \(\rho = 3y\sqrt{x}\).

Spinning about the \(y\)-axis uses \(x^2\): \(\int_0^6 x^{5/2}\,dx\cdot\int_0^2 3y\,dy = \tfrac{432\sqrt6}{7}\cdot 6 = \tfrac{2592\sqrt6}{7} \approx 907.0\), matching the notes. The notes' wording says "\(I_y\) about the \(x\)-axis." \(I_y\) is about the \(y\)-axis, and the printed answer confirms that's what they meant.

Topic 5 · one more dimension

Solids in three dimensions

For a solid \(Q\) with density \(\rho(x,y,z)\) (mass per unit volume), everything carries over with triple integrals. The one new idea is what the distances are measured from.

In 3D, the distance that tells you how far a point sits "in the \(x\) direction" is its distance from the \(yz\)-plane, the wall where \(x = 0\). So the moment that gives \(\bar x\) is the moment about the \(yz\)-plane.

Center of mass of a solid \[m = \iiint_Q \rho\,dV\] \[M_{yz} = \iiint_Q x\rho\,dV \qquad M_{xz} = \iiint_Q y\rho\,dV \qquad M_{xy} = \iiint_Q z\rho\,dV\] \[\bar x = \frac{M_{yz}}{m} \qquad \bar y = \frac{M_{xz}}{m} \qquad \bar z = \frac{M_{xy}}{m}\]

A trick for remembering: the plane in the subscript is missing one letter, and that missing letter is what goes inside and what you find. \(M_{yz}\) is missing \(x\), so it has \(x\rho\) inside and gives \(\bar x\).

Moments of inertia in 3D are about the three axes. The squared distance from \((x,y,z)\) to the \(x\)-axis is \(y^2 + z^2\), since the \(x\) coordinate doesn't move you away from that axis.

Moments of inertia of a solid \[I_x = \iiint_Q (y^2+z^2)\rho\,dV \qquad I_y = \iiint_Q (x^2+z^2)\rho\,dV \qquad I_z = \iiint_Q (x^2+y^2)\rho\,dV\]

Two typos in the notes' 3D box. It prints \(I_z\) with \((y^2+y^2)\); it should be \((x^2+y^2)\). It also calls these "moments of inertia about the \(yz\)-plane, the \(xz\)-plane and the \(xy\)-plane." They are about the \(x\)-, \(y\)- and \(z\)-axes.

Example 5Center of mass of a solidnotes p. 9

Find the center of mass of the solid \(Q = \{(x,y,z) \mid 0 \le x \le 3,\ 0 \le y \le 6,\ 0 \le z \le 6\}\) with density \(\rho(x,y,z) = 4x + 6y + 5z\). Use a calculator to evaluate the integrals.

  1. Mass

    Work inside out. The inner integral is \(\int_0^6 (4x+6y+5z)\,dz = 24x + 36y + 90\). The middle one gives \(144x + 1188\). The outer one gives the mass:

    \[m = \int_0^3\!\!\int_0^6\!\!\int_0^6 (4x+6y+5z)\,dz\,dy\,dx = 4212\]
  2. \(M_{yz}\): put \(x\) in front
    \[M_{yz} = \iiint_Q x(4x+6y+5z)\,dV = 6642\]
  3. \(M_{xz}\): put \(y\) in front
    \[M_{xz} = \iiint_Q y(4x+6y+5z)\,dV = 14580\]
  4. \(M_{xy}\): put \(z\) in front
    \[M_{xy} = \iiint_Q z(4x+6y+5z)\,dV = 14256\]
  5. Divide each moment by \(m\)

    \(\bar x = M_{yz}/m\), \(\bar y = M_{xz}/m\), \(\bar z = M_{xy}/m\). Compute them.

Reveal the answer
\((\bar x, \bar y, \bar z) = \left(\dfrac{41}{26},\ \dfrac{45}{13},\ \dfrac{44}{13}\right) \approx (1.577,\ 3.462,\ 3.385)\)\(6642/4212\), \(14580/4212\), \(14256/4212\), reduced.

Lesson 29 · an arrow at every point

Vector fields

The big idea

A vector field attaches an arrow to every point. Wind maps, river currents and gravity are all vector fields. You'll learn to read them, sketch them, sort them into types, and recognize the special ones that come from the gradient of a single function.

Topic 1 · from first principles

What a vector field is

A vector is an arrow. It has a direction and a length (also called its magnitude). In the plane you write it with two components, \(\langle P, Q\rangle\): go \(P\) units across and \(Q\) units up. The vectors \(\mathbf{i} = \langle 1,0\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{j} = \langle 0,1\rangle\) point along the axes, so \(\langle P,Q\rangle = P\,\mathbf{i} + Q\,\mathbf{j}\).

A function you've used before takes a point and gives back a number. A vector field takes a point and gives back a vector. You draw that vector as an arrow with its tail at the point.

Analogy · a weather map

A wind map shows a small arrow at each city. The arrow points the way the wind blows there, and a longer arrow means a stronger wind. The map is a vector field. A different city gets a different arrow.

Vector field

A vector field \(\mathbf{F}\) in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) assigns a two-dimensional vector \(\mathbf{F}(x,y)\) to each point \((x,y)\) of a set \(D\), called its domain. It has two component functions:

\[\mathbf{F}(x,y) = \langle P(x,y),\ Q(x,y)\rangle = P(x,y)\,\mathbf{i} + Q(x,y)\,\mathbf{j}\]

\(\mathbf{F}\) is continuous when \(P\) and \(Q\) are continuous.

Field explorer · drag the probe

The field is \(\mathbf{F} = \langle ax + by,\ cx + dy\rangle\). Drag the gold probe anywhere to read \(\mathbf{F}\) there. "Unit arrows" draws every arrow the same length so you can see directions only. That's the idea behind unit fields in Topic 4.

Example 1Evaluate a field at a pointnotes p. 3

Let \(\mathbf{F}(x,y) = \dfrac{4x}{y}\,\mathbf{i} + \dfrac{-5x-4y}{x-2y}\,\mathbf{j}\). What vector is attached to the point \((1,-2)\)?

  1. Name the components

    \(P(x,y) = \dfrac{4x}{y}\) and \(Q(x,y) = \dfrac{-5x-4y}{x-2y}\). The answer is \(\langle P(1,-2),\ Q(1,-2)\rangle\).

  2. Plug into \(P\)
    \[P(1,-2) = \frac{4(1)}{-2}\]
  3. Plug into \(Q\)
    \[Q(1,-2) = \frac{-5(1) - 4(-2)}{1 - 2(-2)} = \frac{-5+8}{1+4}\]

    Simplify both and write the vector.

Reveal the answer
\(\mathbf{F}(1,-2) = -2\,\mathbf{i} + \tfrac35\,\mathbf{j} = \langle -2,\ 0.6\rangle\)An arrow starting at \((1,-2)\) that points left and a little up.

Watch the signs: \(-4(-2) = +8\) on top and \(-2(-2) = +4\) on the bottom.

Topic 2 · drawing arrows

Sketching a vector field

To sketch a field by hand:

  1. Pick a handful of easy points, like ones on the axes and the diagonals.
  2. Plug each point into \(\mathbf{F}\) to get its vector.
  3. Draw that vector as an arrow with its tail at the point.
  4. If the arrows are long enough to crash into each other, shrink them all by the same factor. The directions and the relative lengths are what matter.

After a few arrows, a pattern usually jumps out, and you can fill in the rest by eye.

Example 2Sketch a fieldnotes p. 4

Sketch the vector field \(\mathbf{F}(x,y) = \left\langle \dfrac{x}{2},\ \dfrac{y}{2}\right\rangle\).

  1. Pick easy points

    Try \((2,0)\), \((0,2)\), \((-2,0)\), \((0,-2)\), \((2,2)\) and \((-2,-2)\).

  2. Compute their vectors

    Halve each coordinate:

    PointVector
    \((2,0)\)\(\langle 1,0\rangle\)
    \((0,2)\)\(\langle 0,1\rangle\)
    \((-2,0)\)\(\langle -1,0\rangle\)
    \((0,-2)\)\(\langle 0,-1\rangle\)
    \((2,2)\)\(\langle 1,1\rangle\)
    \((-2,-2)\)\(\langle -1,-1\rangle\)
  3. Draw each arrow from its point

    Put the tail of \(\langle 1,0\rangle\) at \((2,0)\), and so on.

  4. Look for the pattern

    Ask two questions. Which way does each arrow point compared with the origin? How does the length change as you move out? Then fill in the rest.

This is a radial field, the first of the two named types in the next topic. At the origin itself the vector is \(\langle 0,0\rangle\), so there's no arrow there.

Topic 3 · two named shapes

Radial and rotational fields

Radial field

\[\mathbf{F} = ax\,\mathbf{i} + ay\,\mathbf{j}\]

Every arrow lies along the line through the origin. If \(a\) is positive, they point away from it. If \(a\) is negative, they point toward it. The length is \(|a|\) times the distance from the origin. Gravity and electric fields from a point charge look like this, though their strength fades with distance.

Rotational field

\[\mathbf{F} = ay\,\mathbf{i} - ax\,\mathbf{j}\]

Every arrow is tangent to the circle centered at the origin through its point. With this form, a positive \(a\) turns clockwise and a negative \(a\) turns counterclockwise. The length again depends only on the distance from the origin. Storms and whirlpools look like this.

Why is the rotational one tangent to circles? Dot it with the position vector \(\langle x, y\rangle\), which points straight out from the origin: \(ay\cdot x + (-ax)\cdot y = 0\). A zero dot product means the two are perpendicular. An arrow perpendicular to the radius runs along the circle.

Why clockwise for positive \(a\)? Check one point. At \((1,0)\), \(\mathbf{F} = \langle 0, -a\rangle\), which points down. Pointing down on the right side of a circle means turning clockwise.

The notes only list the clockwise-for-positive form. The field \(-y\,\mathbf{i} + x\,\mathbf{j}\) is also rotational. It's the same form with \(a = -1\), and it turns counterclockwise.

Example 3Classify three fieldsnotes p. 5

Classify each field as radial, rotational, or neither: (a) \(\mathbf{F} = 2y\,\mathbf{i} - 2x\,\mathbf{j}\), (b) \(\mathbf{F} = 2xy\,\mathbf{i} - 5x\,\mathbf{j}\), (c) \(\mathbf{F} = 5x\,\mathbf{i} + 5y\,\mathbf{j}\).

  1. Keep the two templates in view

    Radial is \(ax\,\mathbf{i} + ay\,\mathbf{j}\): \(x\) in front of \(\mathbf{i}\), \(y\) in front of \(\mathbf{j}\), same number \(a\). Rotational is \(ay\,\mathbf{i} - ax\,\mathbf{j}\): the letters are swapped and the second term gets a minus sign.

  2. Field (a)

    \(P = 2y\) and \(Q = -2x\). Line it up against both templates and see which one fits with a single number \(a\).

  3. Field (b)

    \(P = 2xy\) and \(Q = -5x\). Neither template has a product like \(xy\), and the two numbers 2 and 5 differ.

  4. Field (c)

    \(P = 5x\) and \(Q = 5y\). Compare with the templates the same way.

Reveal the answer
(a) rotational (\(a = 2\), clockwise) · (b) neither · (c) radial (\(a = 5\), pointing outward)(a) matches \(ay\,\mathbf{i} - ax\,\mathbf{j}\). (c) matches \(ax\,\mathbf{i} + ay\,\mathbf{j}\).

In (b), the arrows are neither tangent to circles nor lined up with the origin. At \((1,1)\) it gives \(\langle 2,-5\rangle\), which is neither along \(\langle 1,1\rangle\) nor perpendicular to it.

Topic 4 · direction only

Unit vector fields

A unit vector has length 1. A unit vector field has length 1 at every point of its domain. It keeps the directions of a field and throws away the strengths.

Why would you want that? If you're steering a boat, you may care which way the current flows at each spot and not how fast. A unit field answers exactly that question.

To turn any field into a unit field, divide each vector by its own length. This is called normalizing:

\[\frac{\mathbf{F}}{\|\mathbf{F}\|} = \left\langle \frac{P}{\|\mathbf{F}\|},\ \frac{Q}{\|\mathbf{F}\|},\ \frac{R}{\|\mathbf{F}\|}\right\rangle, \qquad \|\mathbf{F}\| = \sqrt{P^2+Q^2+R^2}\]

Normalizing fails wherever \(\mathbf{F}\) is the zero vector, because you can't divide by zero length.

    \[\|\mathbf{F}\| = \sqrt{y^2 + 16x^2}\]

Topic 5 · three components

Vector fields in \(\mathbb{R}^3\)

Everything above works in space with a third component:

\[\mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = \langle P,\ Q,\ R\rangle = P\,\mathbf{i} + Q\,\mathbf{j} + R\,\mathbf{k}\]

Each of \(P\), \(Q\) and \(R\) is a function of \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\), and \(\mathbf{k} = \langle 0,0,1\rangle\) points up. Space fields model gravity around a planet, heat flowing through a block, and water moving through the whole depth of a river. A 2D field can only show one flat slice of a river, such as its surface.

Quick check: what vector does \(\mathbf{F} = \langle xz,\ y,\ x+z\rangle\) attach to \((1,2,3)\)?

\(\langle 1\cdot 3,\ 2,\ 1+3\rangle = \langle 3, 2, 4\rangle\).

Topic 6 · fields from a function

Gradient fields

You've already met one kind of vector field. The gradient of a function \(f(x,y)\) is the vector of its partial derivatives:

\[\nabla f = \langle f_x,\ f_y\rangle \qquad\text{or in 3D}\qquad \nabla f = \langle f_x,\ f_y,\ f_z\rangle\]

Recall what it means. Picture \(f\) as the height of a landscape. At each point, \(\nabla f\) points in the steepest uphill direction, it crosses the contour line through that point at a right angle, and its length is how steep the ground is. Do that at every point and you have a vector field.

Gradient field

A vector field \(\mathbf{F}\) in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) or \(\mathbb{R}^3\) is a gradient field (also called a conservative field) if there is a scalar function \(f\) with \(\nabla f = \mathbf{F}\).

Analogy · water on a hillside

Turn the gradient arrows around and you get the way rain runs off a hill: straight downhill, crossing every contour line at a right angle, fastest where the slope is steepest. The whole flow pattern comes from one thing, the shape of the land. A gradient field is a field that comes from one height function like that.

Gradient field over a contour map

Brighter means higher. Each arrow is \(\nabla f\) at its point. Drag the gold probe and the gold contour line through it follows. The probe's arrow always crosses that contour at a right angle and points uphill.

Topic 7 · working backward

Potential functions

Potential function

A function \(f\) is a potential function for \(\mathbf{F}\) if \(\nabla f = \mathbf{F}\).

The name comes from physics. Gravity is a conservative field, and its potential function is (minus) the potential energy. Lesson 31 will show why this matters: line integrals of conservative fields become easy.

Checking a proposed potential is easy. Take its gradient and compare, one component at a time. Finding one from scratch is harder, and comes later.

Is a potential function unique?

Not quite. Adding a constant to \(f\) doesn't change any of its partial derivatives, so \(f\) and \(f + 7\) have the same gradient. That's the only freedom you get, as long as the domain is in one piece:

Uniqueness of potential functions

Let \(\mathbf{F}\) be conservative on an open connected domain, with \(\nabla f = \mathbf{F}\) and \(\nabla g = \mathbf{F}\). Then \(f = g + C\) for some constant \(C\).

Connected means you can walk from any point of the domain to any other without leaving it. Open means every point has a little disk around it that's still in the domain, so no boundary edge is included. This mirrors Calculus 1, where two antiderivatives of the same function differ only by a constant.

Topic 8 · a quick test

The cross-partial test

Given a field, how can you tell whether it could be a gradient without hunting for \(f\)? Use mixed partial derivatives.

If \(\mathbf{F} = \langle P, Q\rangle = \nabla f\), then \(P = f_x\) and \(Q = f_y\). Differentiate \(P\) by \(y\) and \(Q\) by \(x\):

\[P_y = f_{xy} \qquad Q_x = f_{yx}\]

Clairaut's theorem says mixed partials are equal when they're continuous, so \(P_y = Q_x\). A gradient field must pass this test.

Cross-partial property of conservative fields

Let \(\mathbf{F}\) have component functions with continuous first partial derivatives on its domain.

  • If \(\mathbf{F} = \langle P, Q\rangle\) is conservative, then \(P_y = Q_x\).
  • If \(\mathbf{F} = \langle P, Q, R\rangle\) is conservative, then \(P_y = Q_x\), \(Q_z = R_y\) and \(R_x = P_z\).

The test only works in one direction. If the cross partials differ, the field is definitely not conservative. If they match, the field could be conservative, but this test alone doesn't prove it. (The domain matters too, and later lessons cover that.)

Analogy · a paddle wheel extra, not in notes

Drop a tiny paddle wheel into a flowing field. If the flow pushes one side of the wheel harder than the other, the wheel spins. The difference \(Q_x - P_y\) measures exactly that spin. A gradient field is "downhill flow", and downhill flow never spins a paddle wheel. You'll meet this quantity again as curl.

Lesson 30 · adding up along a curve

Line integrals

The big idea

A line integral adds something up along a curve instead of along the \(x\)-axis. The scalar kind finds the area of a curtain hanging between a curve and a surface. The vector kind measures how much a field pushes along your path (work), across it (flux), or all the way around a loop (circulation).

Topic 1 · the tool you need first

Riding a curve: parameterizations, speed and \(ds\)

A parameterization describes a curve \(C\) as the path of a moving point. At time \(t\), the point is at

\[\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle x(t),\ y(t),\ z(t)\rangle, \qquad a \le t \le b\]

Its derivative \(\mathbf{r}'(t)\) is the velocity. It points along the curve, the way you're moving. Its length \(\|\mathbf{r}'(t)\|\) is the speed.

Distance traveled is speed × time. In a tiny time \(dt\), the point covers a tiny length of curve:

\[ds = \|\mathbf{r}'(t)\|\,dt\]

This one line is the bridge for this whole lesson. Every line integral is written with \(ds\) (a bit of length), and you compute it with \(dt\) (a bit of time). The speed converts one into the other.

Analogy · a car's odometer

Your odometer doesn't care how you steer. It counts distance. If the speedometer reads 60 mph for one minute, the odometer adds one mile. Speed × time = distance is all \(ds = \|\mathbf{r}'\|\,dt\) says.

Topic 2 · a curtain under a surface

Scalar line integrals

In Calculus 1, \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) adds up \(f\) along a stretch of the \(x\)-axis. A scalar line integral adds up a function \(f\) along a curve \(C\) instead.

  1. Chop the curve

    Split \(C\) into \(n\) small pieces. Piece \(i\) has length \(\Delta s_i\).

  2. Sample

    Pick a point \(P_i^*\) on each piece and evaluate \(f(P_i^*)\).

  3. Multiply and add

    Each product \(f(P_i^*)\,\Delta s_i\) is the area of a thin panel standing on that piece. Add them all: \(\sum f(P_i^*)\,\Delta s_i\).

  4. Refine

    Let the pieces shrink. The limit is the line integral.

Scalar line integral \[\int_C f\,ds = \lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n} f(P_i^*)\,\Delta s_i\]

This works the same way for a curve in the plane with \(f(x,y)\) or a curve in space with \(f(x,y,z)\).

Here's the picture when \(f \ge 0\) on a plane curve. Lift \(C\) straight up onto the surface \(z = f(x,y)\). Then hang a curtain from that lifted curve down to the floor. The curtain's area is \(\int_C f\,ds\). Where \(f\) is negative, the curtain hangs below the floor and that part counts as negative area, just like in Calculus 1.

Analogy · painting a fence

A fence follows a winding property line, and its height changes as it goes. The paint you need depends on the fence's area: height × length, added up along the line. That's a scalar line integral, with \(f\) as the height and \(C\) as the property line.

Evaluating a scalar line integral

If \(f\) is continuous and \(C\) is a smooth curve with parameterization \(\mathbf{r}(t)\), \(a \le t \le b\), then

\[\int_C f\,ds = \int_a^b f(\mathbf{r}(t))\,\|\mathbf{r}'(t)\|\,dt\]

Recipe: plug the curve into \(f\), multiply by the speed, and integrate in \(t\).

Topic 3 · the simplest curtain

Arc length as a line integral

Set \(f = 1\). The curtain is then exactly 1 unit tall everywhere, so its area equals the length of its base. That base is the curve:

\[\text{Arc length} = \int_C 1\,ds = \int_a^b \|\mathbf{r}'(t)\|\,dt\]

It's the odometer again: add up speed × time.

Topic 4 · fields along a path

Vector line integrals: work

Now let a vector field \(\mathbf{F}\), like a force or a wind, act on a particle moving along \(C\). How much work does the field do?

From physics, the work done by a constant force over a straight move is force × distance, counting only the part of the force that points along the move. Push straight against a wall and you do no work on a cart rolling beside it. That "part along the move" is a dot product with the unit tangent vector \(\mathbf{T}\), which points the way you're going:

\[\text{work on one small piece} \approx \mathbf{F}(P_i^*)\cdot\mathbf{T}(P_i^*)\,\Delta s_i\]
Analogy · biking in the wind

A tailwind pushes you along and helps. A headwind fights you. A crosswind blowing straight across your path neither helps nor hurts your forward progress. Only the part of the wind along your direction of travel counts.

Direction matters

Riding the same road the other way turns a tailwind into a headwind. So a vector line integral needs a chosen direction along \(C\), called its orientation. A curve with a chosen direction is an oriented curve. Reversing the orientation flips the sign of the answer.

A closed curve ends where it starts. It has a parameterization with \(\mathbf{r}(a) = \mathbf{r}(b)\) that traces it exactly once, like a loop around a track.

Vector line integral \[\int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{T}\,ds = \lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{F}(P_i^*)\cdot\mathbf{T}(P_i^*)\,\Delta s_i\]

Now convert to \(t\). The unit tangent is \(\mathbf{T} = \dfrac{\mathbf{r}'(t)}{\|\mathbf{r}'(t)\|}\) and \(ds = \|\mathbf{r}'(t)\|\,dt\). The two speeds cancel:

\[\int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{T}\,ds = \int_a^b \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t))\cdot\frac{\mathbf{r}'(t)}{\|\mathbf{r}'(t)\|}\,\|\mathbf{r}'(t)\|\,dt = \int_a^b \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t))\cdot\mathbf{r}'(t)\,dt\]

The short name for this is \(\displaystyle\int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\), where \(d\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}'(t)\,dt\).

Recipe: plug the curve into \(\mathbf{F}\), dot with the velocity \(\mathbf{r}'(t)\), and integrate in \(t\). No square roots needed.

Line lab · along or across

Green stretches of path add to the total and red stretches subtract. The gold arrow is \(\mathbf{F}\) at the bead. The blue arrow is the unit direction you're measuring against: \(\mathbf{T}\) for work, or the normal \(\mathbf{N}\) for flux. Their dot product is the bar's height on the right. Reverse the direction and every color flips.

Topic 5 · another way to write it

The expanded form \(P\,dx + Q\,dy + R\,dz\)

Write the field as \(\mathbf{F} = \langle P, Q, R\rangle\) and the small step along the curve as \(d\mathbf{r} = \langle dx, dy, dz\rangle\). Their dot product is

\[\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = P\,dx + Q\,dy + R\,dz \qquad\text{so}\qquad \int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_C P\,dx + Q\,dy + R\,dz\]

Same integral, different handwriting. To compute it, write everything in terms of \(t\). Replace \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) with their formulas, and replace each differential using the derivative: \(dx = x'(t)\,dt\), \(dy = y'(t)\,dt\), \(dz = z'(t)\,dt\).

When you see \(\int_C P\,dx + Q\,dy + R\,dz\), read off \(\mathbf{F} = \langle P, Q, R\rangle\). It's a work integral.

Example 6Expanded formnotes p. 10

Find \(\displaystyle\int_C z\,dx + x\,dy + y\,dz\), where \(C\) is \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle t^2,\ \sqrt t,\ t\rangle\), \(1 \le t \le 4\).

  1. Read off the pieces

    The field is \(\mathbf{F} = \langle z, x, y\rangle\). On the curve, \(x = t^2\), \(y = \sqrt t\) and \(z = t\).

  2. Differentials

    \(dx = 2t\,dt\), \(dy = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt t}\,dt\), and \(dz = dt\).

  3. Substitute
    \[z\,dx = t\cdot 2t\,dt = 2t^2\,dt \qquad x\,dy = t^2\cdot\frac{1}{2\sqrt t}\,dt = \tfrac12 t^{3/2}\,dt \qquad y\,dz = t^{1/2}\,dt\]
  4. Integrate each from 1 to 4
    \[\int_1^4 2t^2\,dt = 42 \qquad \int_1^4 \tfrac12 t^{3/2}\,dt = \tfrac15\Big[t^{5/2}\Big]_1^4 = \tfrac{31}{5} \qquad \int_1^4 t^{1/2}\,dt = \tfrac23\Big[t^{3/2}\Big]_1^4 = \tfrac{14}{3}\]

    Add the three.

Reveal the answer
\(\displaystyle\int_C z\,dx + x\,dy + y\,dz = \frac{793}{15} \approx 52.87\)\(42 + \tfrac{31}{5} + \tfrac{14}{3} = \tfrac{630 + 93 + 70}{15}\).

Almost all of it comes from \(z\,dx\), because the curve moves 15 units in \(x\) and only 1 in \(y\) and 3 in \(z\).

Quick check (notes Practice 1): \(\int_C -24x^2yz\,dx - 5z\,dy - 8xy\,dz\) on \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle t, t^2, t^3\rangle\), \(0 \le t \le 1\).

With \(dx = dt\), \(dy = 2t\,dt\), \(dz = 3t^2\,dt\), the integrand is \(-24t^7 - 10t^4 - 24t^5\). Its integral is \(-3 - 2 - 4 = -9\). The notes print \(9\), but every term is negative on \(0 \le t \le 1\), so the answer must be negative. Running the curve backward would give \(+9\), so the notes likely dropped the sign.

Topic 6 · flow across a curve

Flux

Work measures how much a field pushes along a curve. Flux measures how much it flows across it. Swap the unit tangent \(\mathbf{T}\) for a unit normal \(\mathbf{N}\), which points straight across the curve:

\[\text{Flux} = \int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{N}\,ds\]

If \(\mathbf{F}\) is the velocity of a fluid and \(C\) is a screen or membrane, the flux is how much fluid passes through the screen per unit time.

Analogy · a fishing net in a river

Hold a net across a river and lots of water passes through it. Turn the net so it lines up with the current and almost nothing passes through. Water running along the net doesn't count. Only the part of the flow aimed across the net does.

Which side counts as "across"? The notes use the normal \(\mathbf{n}(t) = \langle y'(t),\ -x'(t)\rangle\). That's the velocity turned a quarter turn clockwise, so it points to the right of the direction of travel. For a loop traced counterclockwise, the right side is the outside, so positive flux means flow outward.

Flux across a curve

Let \(C\) be smooth with parameterization \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle x(t), y(t)\rangle\), \(a \le t \le b\), and let \(\mathbf{n}(t) = \langle y'(t),\ -x'(t)\rangle\). The flux of \(\mathbf{F}\) across \(C\) is

\[\int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{N}\,ds = \int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot\frac{\mathbf{n}(t)}{\|\mathbf{n}(t)\|}\,ds = \int_a^b \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t))\cdot\mathbf{n}(t)\,dt\]

The same cancellation happens as before: \(\|\mathbf{n}\| = \|\mathbf{r}'\|\), so it cancels with the speed in \(ds\).

Line lab · flux across a loop

The same lab, set to measure flow across. The blue arrow is now \(\mathbf{N}\), pointing to the right of travel. An outward field crossing a counterclockwise loop gives positive flux. Try "Swirl": it flows along the loop, never across, and the flux is zero.

Example 7Flux across a circlenotes p. 12

Calculate the flux of \(\mathbf{F} = \langle -x,\ -y\rangle\) across the circle \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle -3\cos t,\ -3\sin t\rangle\), \(0 \le t \le 2\pi\).

  1. Which way does it travel?

    \(\mathbf{r}(0) = (-3, 0)\) and \(\mathbf{r}(\tfrac{\pi}{2}) = (0, -3)\). Going from the left side to the bottom is counterclockwise. So the right of travel is the outside.

  2. Velocity and normal

    \(x' = 3\sin t\) and \(y' = -3\cos t\), so

    \[\mathbf{n}(t) = \langle y',\ -x'\rangle = \langle -3\cos t,\ -3\sin t\rangle\]

    At \(t = 0\) that's \(\langle -3, 0\rangle\), pointing left, away from the center. Outward, as expected.

  3. Plug the curve into \(\mathbf{F}\)
    \[\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) = \langle 3\cos t,\ 3\sin t\rangle\]

    This points toward the center. The fluid drains inward.

  4. Dot them
    \[\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n} = -9\cos^2 t - 9\sin^2 t = -9\]
  5. Integrate
    \[\int_0^{2\pi} -9\,dt\]

    Evaluate.

Reveal the answer
\(\displaystyle\int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{N}\,ds = -18\pi \approx -56.55\)\(-9\cdot 2\pi\).

Negative flux means net flow is inward, against the outward normal. Check it another way: on the circle the field has strength 3 and points straight in, and the circle's length is \(6\pi\). So \(-3\cdot 6\pi = -18\pi\).

Quick check (notes Practice 2): flux of \(\langle -5y,\ 2x\rangle\) across \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle t, t^2\rangle\), \(0 \le t \le 2\), to the right of travel.

\(\mathbf{n} = \langle 2t, -1\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{F} = \langle -5t^2, 2t\rangle\), so \(\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n} = -10t^3 - 2t\). Integrating from 0 to 2 gives \(-40 - 4 = -44\), matching the notes.

Topic 7 · around a loop

Circulation

When \(C\) is a closed curve, the work integral has its own name, circulation, and its own symbol with a circle on the integral sign:

\[\oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{T}\,ds = \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\]

The circle reminds you that \(C\) has no endpoints. Circulation measures how much the field tends to carry you around the loop. A whirlpool has a lot of circulation. A steady wind has none, because what it gives on one side of the loop it takes back on the other.

A closed curve still has a direction. Circulation counterclockwise and circulation clockwise have opposite signs. If a problem doesn't say, the convention is counterclockwise.

Example 8Circulation around a circlenotes p. 12

Let \(\mathbf{F} = \langle 2y,\ -2x\rangle\) and let \(C\) be the unit circle oriented counterclockwise. Calculate the circulation of \(\mathbf{F}\) along \(C\).

  1. Parameterize the circle

    The standard counterclockwise unit circle is \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle \cos t,\ \sin t\rangle\), \(0 \le t \le 2\pi\).

  2. Plug the curve into \(\mathbf{F}\)
    \[\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) = \langle 2\sin t,\ -2\cos t\rangle\]
  3. Velocity

    \(\mathbf{r}'(t) = \langle -\sin t,\ \cos t\rangle\).

  4. Dot them
    \[\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{r}' = -2\sin^2 t - 2\cos^2 t = -2\]
  5. Integrate around once
    \[\oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_0^{2\pi} -2\,dt\]

    Evaluate.

Reveal the answer
\(\displaystyle\oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = -4\pi \approx -12.57\)\(-2\cdot 2\pi\).

This is the rotational field with \(a = 2\), which turns clockwise. You walk counterclockwise, straight into it the whole way, so the circulation is negative.

Quick check (notes Practice 3): circulation of \(\langle -y,\ x\rangle\) around the unit circle, counterclockwise.

\(\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{r}' = \sin^2 t + \cos^2 t = 1\), so the circulation is \(2\pi\), matching the notes. This field turns counterclockwise, with you.

Common mistakes

  • Swapping the moments. \(M_x\) has \(y\) inside, \(M_y\) has \(x\) inside. Same for \(I_x\) and \(I_y\).
  • Dividing by the wrong thing. \(\bar x = M_y/m\), and \(m\) is the mass, not the area, unless the density is 1.
  • Forgetting the density inside a moment. \(M_y = \iint x\rho\,dA\), not \(\iint x\,dA\).
  • Using \(y^2\) for \(I_z\) of a solid twice over, as the notes' typo does. It's \(x^2 + y^2\).
  • Drawing an arrow from the origin instead of from its point. Each arrow's tail sits at the point it belongs to.
  • Treating equal cross partials as proof. They only allow a conservative field.
  • Forgetting the speed \(\|\mathbf{r}'(t)\|\) in a scalar line integral, or adding one to a work integral, where it cancels.
  • Ignoring orientation. Reversing a path flips the sign of work, flux and circulation. Scalar line integrals don't care about direction.
  • Forgetting to change the limits after a substitution like \(u = 1 + \cos^2 t\).
