Start here · the whole MOSFET in three layers

The mental model

What a MOSFET is. A strip of silicon (the channel) between two terminals, the source and the drain, with a third terminal, the gate, sitting on top of it behind a thin insulating oxide. The gate never touches the channel. It works by its electric field alone.

Why we care. Because the gate draws no steady current, a tiny signal can control a big current. That one property makes MOSFETs the switches in every power supply and motor driver, and the logic in every chip: a laptop processor holds billions of them.

The whole device fits in one sentence, and every number on this page comes from it:

The gate is one plate of a capacitor and the channel is the other. The gate's voltage decides how much charge sits in the channel, and the drain's voltage pushes that charge across. Current is charge times speed.

Layer 1 · what it's made of

Construction sets β and VT

The oxide's thickness sets the capacitance per area, Cox. The carriers' mobility μ sets how fast that charge moves. The shape W/L says how much channel there is. Together they make one number, the device strength β = μCoxW/L. Doping and the gate material set the threshold VT.

Layer 2 · its two voltages

VGS fills it, VDS drains it

Past threshold, every volt of VGS adds Cox × 1 V of mobile charge per area. VDS drags it to the drain. While the channel reaches the drain the device acts like a resistor (triode). Once VDS passes VGS − VT, the channel pinches off and the current levels out (saturation).

Layer 3 · the circuit around it

The circuit decides where you sit

A MOSFET alone is a family of curves. The resistor or load around it adds one more line, and the circuit settles where the two cross. A switch sits at the bottom left, an amplifier in the middle of saturation, a current mirror out on the flat part.

Go deeper: Sah's equations from "charge times speed"

Take a slice of channel at position x, where the channel sits at voltage V(x) above the source. The gate's grip there is VGS − V(x), and only the part past threshold makes mobile charge:

\[Q_n(x) = C_{ox}\,\big(V_{GS} - V_T - V(x)\big)\quad\text{coulombs per m}^2\]

Carriers drift at v = μE = μ dV/dx. The current is the charge per length (W Qn) times that speed, and it's the same at every x:

\[I_D = W\,Q_n(x)\,\mu\frac{dV}{dx} = \mu C_{ox} W\,\big(V_{GS} - V_T - V\big)\frac{dV}{dx}\]

Multiply by dx and add up the slices from the source (V = 0) to the drain (V = VDS):

\[I_D\,L = \mu C_{ox}W\left[(V_{GS} - V_T)V_{DS} - \tfrac{V_{DS}^2}{2}\right]\ \Rightarrow\ I_D = \beta\left[(V_{GS} - V_T)V_{DS} - \tfrac{V_{DS}^2}{2}\right]\]

That's Sah's triode equation, with β = μCoxW/L. At VDS = VGS − VT the charge at the drain end reaches zero. Past that point the channel can't pass any more, so the current stays at its peak, (β/2)(VGS − VT)²: the square law. The workbench's "inside the channel" plot draws V(x) and Qn(x) live.

    Reading the workbench

    Every picture shows the same operating point from a different side. Learning to jump between them is most of what "understanding a MOSFET" means.

    Cross-section ↔ profile

    The shaded sheet in the cross-section and the shaded area in "inside the channel" are the same thing: the channel's charge, point by point. When one pinches off, so does the other.

    Output curves ↔ region map

    A vertical line on the region map, at one VGS, is one curve on the output plot. The map's boundary line is the output plot's dashed parabola.

    Transfer ↔ output

    A vertical line on the output plot, at one VDS, gives the whole transfer curve. Their slopes at the dot are gm and 1/ro.

    Numbers ↔ pictures

    Each readout measures a picture: ID is the dot's height, gm the transfer slope, τ the carriers' trip time, N the shaded area. Click one to see which.

    Part 2 · change one thing, watch everything

    Experiments

    The big idea

    Each card sets the workbench up, often pinning a copy for comparison, then changes one thing. Predict what will happen before you look, then check.

    1 · threshold

    Grow a channel

    Setup. VDS is held at 0.3 V while VGS sweeps up from 0.

    Watch. Below VT the gate only pushes holes away, so the depleted layer under the oxide deepens. At VT electrons gather against the oxide and the transfer curve leaves zero.

    Why. Below threshold, the gate's charge is matched by fixed ions, which can't move. Above it, each extra volt adds Cox × 1 V of mobile electrons, and only those carry current.

    2 · saturation

    Pinch it off

    Setup. VGS = 4 V, and VDS sweeps up from 0.

    Watch. The channel's charge thins at the drain end until, at VDS = VGS − VT, it touches zero. From then on the output curve is flat.

    Why. Once the drain end is pinched, more VDS only widens the depleted gap. The channel itself still drops exactly Vov, so it carries the same current.

    3 · two personalities

    Resistor or current source?

    Setup. VGS = 4 V, VDS = 0.25 V: deep triode.

    Watch. "Resistance V/I" and ro are close, as they would be for a resistor. Now drag VDS past 2.5 V: ro jumps to infinity (this part has λ = 0).

    Why. In triode the curve is a sloped line through the origin. In saturation it's flat. One device, two jobs, picked by VDS alone.

    4 · shape

    Double the width

    Setup. Pins the device, then doubles W.

    Watch. Every current doubles at the same voltages; the dashed pinned curve sits at half. β doubles and the resistance halves.

    Why. Twice the width is two channels side by side. That's why a power MOSFET is about a metre wide: try the power preset and read W.

    5 · shape

    Halve the length

    Setup. The textbook device, pinned, then L is halved.

    Watch. β doubles again, but fT goes up four times, and the "square law still accurate?" check starts to climb.

    Why. It's a shorter trip at a steeper field: the transit time goes as L², so speed goes as 1/L². The steeper field also pushes carriers toward their speed limit.

    6 · materials

    Thin the oxide

    Setup. The textbook device, pinned, then tox is halved.

    Watch. Cox and β double. So does the oxide field: watch its row in the constraints.

    Why. A thinner insulator is a bigger capacitor, so more charge per volt. The same voltage across half the thickness is twice the field, which is why thinner oxides forced lower supply voltages.

    7 · temperature

    Heat it up

    Setup. The practice part at 400 K (127 °C). The transfer plot also shows it at 300 K, dashed.

    Watch. At low VGS the hot part conducts more; at high VGS, less. The curves cross at one gate voltage.

    Why. VT falls with heat (more current) while mobility falls (less current). Above the crossing a hot MOSFET carries less, so paralleled MOSFETs share current instead of one hogging it.

    8 · leakage

    The leakage floor

    Setup. The 180 nm chip transistor, below threshold, on a log plot.

    Watch. Below VT the curve is a straight line on the log scale: a fixed number of millivolts per tenfold change. Raise n toward 2 and the off-current at VGS = 0 climbs.

    Why. Carriers hop the barrier thermally, so the current is exponential in VGS, like a diode's. With VT only 0.45 V, "off" sits just a few decades below "on".

    9 · polarity

    n versus p

    Setup. Pins the n-channel part, then switches to its p-channel partner of the same size.

    Watch. At the same voltages the p part carries well under half the current. Holes flow (open circles) instead of electrons, and every voltage is measured from the source.

    Why. Holes are less mobile than electrons: 200 against 500 cm²/V·s here. CMOS chips make their p transistors 2 to 3 times wider to make up for it.

    10 · small signal

    Cheap gain

    Setup. The textbook device at a small overdrive, 0.15 V.

    Watch. gm/ID is about 13 per volt. Drag VGS up to 1.7 V and it falls to about 2 per volt.

    Why. gm = 2ID/Vov, so a small overdrive buys a lot of gain for little current. Analog designers choose a bias point by its gm/ID.

    11 · limits

    Break it

    Setup. The small SOT-23 part at VGS = 4 V, VDS = 12 V.

    Watch. The constraints light up red: the current and the heat are both far past the ratings, and the region map is hatched where you're sitting.

    Why. The square law will happily promise an amp. The part melts at 350 mW. Ratings are physics the equations leave out.

    12 · analog design

    Long channels for gain

    Setup. Pins the 180 nm transistor, then makes it 4 times longer and 4 times wider (the same β), with λ 4 times smaller.

    Watch. The currents barely change, but ro and the intrinsic gain gmro rise about fourfold.

    Why. λ goes roughly as 1/L, because the pinched-off gap is a smaller share of a long channel. So analog circuits use long transistors even in a fast process.

    Part 4 · one idea per card

    Concepts

    The gate

    A capacitor that makes charge

    What. The gate, the oxide and the channel form a capacitor with Cox = εox/tox per area. Past threshold, the channel side fills with mobile carriers.

    Why. It's the whole trick: the gate controls charge without any current flowing into it. Once it's charged, a MOSFET's gate draws nothing.

    \(Q_n = C_{ox}(V_{GS} - V_T)\)
    Threshold

    Where the channel starts

    What. VT is the gate voltage needed to first pull a layer of carriers against the oxide. Below it the gate only empties the silicon of its own carriers.

    Why. It's the switch's 'on' line. Logic-level parts have a VT near 1 to 2 V so a 3.3 V or 5 V signal can drive them hard.

    \(V_{GS} > V_T\ \Rightarrow\ \text{a channel}\)
    Current

    Charge times speed

    What. Current is the charge per length times how fast it moves. Carriers drift at v = μE, so the current depends on both how much charge the gate holds and how hard the drain pulls.

    Why. Every formula on this page is this one, added up along the channel. Remember it and you can rebuild the rest.

    \(I_D = W\,Q_n\,\mu\,\dfrac{dV}{dx}\)
    Triode

    A voltage-controlled resistor

    What. While VDS < VGS − VT, the channel reaches the drain, and near VDS = 0 it's simply a resistor of 1/(βVov).

    Why. Every MOSFET switch lives here when it's on. More gate drive means lower resistance and less heat.

    \(I_D = \beta\big[V_{ov}V_{DS} - \tfrac{V_{DS}^2}{2}\big]\)
    Saturation

    A gate-controlled current source

    What. Once VDS passes Vov the channel pinches off at the drain end, and the current stops depending on VDS.

    Why. A current set by the gate alone is what an amplifier needs: change VGS a little and the current follows, whatever the drain is doing.

    \(I_D = \tfrac{\beta}{2}V_{ov}^2\)
    λ

    Channel-length modulation

    What. In saturation, more VDS moves the pinch-off point toward the source a little. The channel gets shorter, so the current creeps up.

    Why. It gives ro a finite value, which caps an amplifier's gain and makes current mirrors imperfect. Short channels suffer most.

    \(I_D = \tfrac{\beta}{2}V_{ov}^2(1 + \lambda V_{DS})\)
    Below threshold

    Subthreshold conduction

    What. Below VT the current doesn't stop. It falls exponentially, tenfold for every S = n(kT/q)ln 10, which is at least 60 mV at room temperature.

    Why. It's the leakage that drains a phone's battery while it sleeps, and it's why supply voltages stopped shrinking near 1 V.

    \(I_D \propto e^{(V_{GS} - V_T)/(n kT/q)}\)
    Heat

    Temperature

    What. Heating lowers VT by about 2 mV/K and lowers mobility as T−1.5. At high gate drive the mobility effect wins and the current falls.

    Why. Because a hot power MOSFET carries less current, parallel parts balance themselves instead of running away. A bipolar transistor does the opposite.

    \(\mu \propto T^{-1.5},\ \ dV_T/dT \approx -2\,\text{mV/K}\)
    Small signal

    gm and ro

    What. gm is how much current a small gate wiggle makes; ro is how little a drain wiggle matters. They're the two slopes at the operating point.

    Why. An amplifier's gain is gm times whatever resistance the drain sees, and gmro is the most one transistor can give.

    \(g_m = \tfrac{2I_D}{V_{ov}},\ \ r_o \approx \tfrac{1}{\lambda I_D}\)
    Speed

    Capacitance and fT

    What. The gate is a capacitor of about WLCox that has to be charged to switch. fT = gm/(2πCgs) measures how fast the device can respond.

    Why. It's why gate drivers exist and why switching wastes energy at every edge. Shrinking L raises fT as 1/L².

    \(f_T \approx \tfrac{3\mu V_{ov}}{4\pi L^2}\)
    Limits

    Ratings and the safe operating area

    What. VDS(max), VGS(max), ID(max) and Pmax fence off the voltages and currents a part survives. Together they draw its safe operating area.

    Why. Sah's equations know nothing about breakdown or heat. Every real design checks the operating point against these four.

    \(V_{DS}\,I_D \le P_{max}\)
    Polarity

    The p-channel mirror

    What. A p-channel part is the same device with every sign flipped. Measured from the source, VSG, VSD, |VT| and |ID| obey the same equations.

    Why. It turns on when its gate goes low, which makes high-side switches and CMOS logic possible. It's weaker, because holes are slower.

    \(|I_D| = \tfrac{\beta_p}{2}(V_{SG} - |V_T|)^2\)

    Everything on one screen

    Cheat sheet

    Which region?
    \(V_{GS} \le V_T\): cutoff
    \(V_{DS} < V_{GS} - V_T\): triode
    \(V_{DS} \ge V_{GS} - V_T\): saturation

    Always check this first.

    Triode
    \(I_D = \beta\big[(V_{GS} - V_T)V_{DS} - \tfrac{V_{DS}^2}{2}\big]\)

    A resistor of about \(1/(\beta V_{ov})\) near \(V_{DS} = 0\).

    Saturation
    \(I_D = \tfrac{\beta}{2}(V_{GS} - V_T)^2(1 + \lambda V_{DS})\)

    A current source set by the gate.

    Strength
    \(\beta = \mu C_{ox}\tfrac{W}{L},\ \ C_{ox} = \tfrac{3.9\,\varepsilon_0}{t_{ox}}\)

    One datasheet point in saturation gives \(\beta = 2I_D/V_{ov}^2\).

    Slopes
    \(g_m = \beta V_{ov} = \tfrac{2I_D}{V_{ov}}\)
    \(r_o = \tfrac{1/\lambda + V_{DS}}{I_D}\)

    A common-source stage's gain is \(-g_m(R_D \parallel r_o)\).

    p-channel
    \(V_{GS} \to V_{SG},\ V_{DS} \to V_{SD}\)
    \(V_T \to |V_T|,\ I_D \to |I_D|\)

    Then the same formulas. \(I_D\) itself is negative.

    Below threshold
    \(S = n\tfrac{kT}{q}\ln 10 \ge 60\ \text{mV/dec}\)

    At 300 K, \(kT/q = 25.9\) mV.

    Speed
    \(\tau = \tfrac{4L^2}{3\mu V_{ov}},\ \ f_T = \tfrac{g_m}{2\pi C_{gs}}\)

    \(C_{gs} \approx \tfrac23 WLC_{ox}\) in saturation.

    What the model leaves out

    The workbench runs SPICE's level-1 model: Sah's equations with λ, the same equations as the homework. On top of that it can add LTspice's subthreshold smoothing and simple temperature rules. That's enough to build intuition, but real parts do more:

    • Velocity saturation and mobility degradation. Short channels and strong fields make real currents smaller than the square law says. The workbench flags when this matters but doesn't apply it.
    • The body effect. Here the body is always tied to the source. When it isn't, VT rises with the source-to-body voltage.
    • Capacitances. They appear only in the switching-transient circuit, as fixed fractions of WLCox. Real ones change with voltage.
    • Breakdown and heating. The ratings are drawn and checked, but nothing actually breaks, and the die's temperature doesn't feed back into the current.
