Followers, Darlingtons and Regulators

Last week's amplifier made a small voltage bigger. This week's circuits do a transistor's other job. Start here · no prior knowledge needed

The big picture: voltage and muscle

Every signal comes from something: a sensor, a microphone, an amplifier stage. Each one can make a voltage. Each one also has some resistance inside it, and that inside resistance limits how much current it can push out.

A heavy load is one with low resistance, like a 100 Ω speaker or a 10 Ω heater. It wants a lot of current. Connect one to a weak source and the source's inside resistance and the load form a voltage divider. Most of the voltage is lost inside the source before it reaches the load. That's the "Wire it straight" setting above.

Every problem this week fixes that the same way. A transistor goes between the source and the load. It copies the voltage, and it fetches the current from a power supply, so the source only has to supply a trickle.

  • Problem 1 is one transistor doing that job, a circuit called an emitter follower.
  • Problem 2 puts a follower after last week's kind of amplifier, so its big gain survives a 150 Ω load.
  • Problem 3 stacks two followers into a Darlington pair, for loads that need amps.
  • Problem 4 puts a Darlington under an op-amp's control, which makes a voltage regulator.
Analogy · a porter

You walk through an airport and choose the route. A porter follows a step behind, pushing the cart with every suitcase. Your legs set the path, and the porter's arms carry the weight. In these circuits the input sets the voltage, and the transistor carries the current, using energy from the power supply.

A follower keeps the voltage and multiplies the current the source can deliver.

Start here · the only two rules you need

Two transistor rules you'll use all page

A bipolar junction transistor (BJT) has three legs: the base, the collector and the emitter. Every transistor here is an NPN type, drawn with an arrow pointing out of the emitter. Current goes in at the collector and the base, and all of it comes out of the emitter.

Rule 1 · a small base current controls a big one. A small current into the base lets a much bigger current flow from collector to emitter. The ratio is the current gain β (beta), usually somewhere from 50 to 300, so \(I_C = \beta I_B\). The emitter carries both currents, so \(I_E = I_C + I_B = (\beta + 1) I_B\).

β is big, so β and β + 1 differ by about 1%. This course usually takes the shortcut \(I_E \approx I_C\), which means \(I_B \approx I_E/\beta\). Problems 1c, 3 and 4c show both versions, and they never differ by more than 2%.

Rule 2 · the base-emitter junction is a diode. When it conducts, it holds about 0.7 V. So the emitter always sits about 0.7 V below the base.

\[I_E = (\beta + 1)\,I_B \approx \beta\,I_B \qquad\qquad V_E = V_B - 0.7\text{ V}\]

The emitter sits 0.7 V below the base and carries about β times the base current.

Quick check: a base sits at 3.0 V. Where's the emitter?

At 2.3 V, one diode drop below.

Quick check: with \(I_B\) = 20 µA and β = 150, about how much current leaves the emitter?

About 3.0 mA, from 150 × 20 µA. The exact β + 1 version gives 3.02 mA.

Problem 1 · 8 pts

The emitter follower

The big idea

Take the output from the emitter instead of the collector. The output then copies the input almost exactly, and the transistor supplies about β times more current than the source does.

Topic 1 · from first principles

Why it's called a follower

In Problem 1's circuit, the input goes to the base. The collector connects straight to the 15 V supply. The output comes off the emitter, through a capacitor C1, to a load resistor RL.

Rule 2 says the emitter sits 0.7 V below the base. Raise the base by 1 V and the emitter rises by about 1 V. Lower it and the emitter follows it down. That's where the name comes from. The voltage gain, the output's change divided by the input's change, comes out just under 1.

What a follower adds is current. The load's current flows from the 15 V supply, in at the collector and out of the emitter. The input supplies only the base current, which is about β times smaller. A follower amplifies current and leaves the voltage alone.

The collector sits at a fixed supply voltage, so it never wiggles. For the signal it acts like ground, which gives the circuit its other name, common collector (CC).

Analogy · a forklift

A forklift driver nudges a lever an inch, and the forks lift a ton of bricks to exactly the height the lever asks for. The lever sets the height, and the engine does the lifting. A follower's base sets the voltage, and the 15 V supply provides the current.

Output follows input, the gain is just under 1, and the supply provides the load's current.

Topic 2 · DC and AC

Draw the circuit twice: DC and AC

A signal in these circuits rides on top of a steady voltage. The steady part is the DC bias, which keeps the transistor partly on. The AC signal is the small wiggle on top. In Problem 1 the base sits at 6.1 V DC, and the signal wiggles it a little above and below that.

The two parts follow different rules, so you work them out separately, in two drawings of the same circuit.

  • The DC view. Capacitors are open circuits, because a charged capacitor blocks steady current. Anything past C1 drops out.
  • The AC view. Capacitors are wires, because the big coupling capacitors pass a wiggle easily. DC supplies become ground, because a voltage that never changes has no wiggle.
Analogy · two photos of a busy road

A long-exposure photo blurs the cars into the steady flow of traffic. A fast snapshot freezes one car's wobble in its lane. It's the same road, with two pictures answering two questions. DC is the long exposure and AC is the snapshot.

In the AC view, look at the follower's emitter. The signal has two ways to reach ground: down through RE, or across C1, which is now a wire, and down through RL. Two resistors that run from the same node to ground are in parallel. Each one gives the current another way out, so together they resist less than either one alone.

\[r_e = R_E \parallel R_L = \frac{R_E\,R_L}{R_E + R_L}\]

This is \(r_e\), the external AC emitter resistance, written without a prime.

DC view: capacitors are open. AC view: capacitors are wires and supplies are ground.

Topic 3 · inside the transistor

The transistor's own resistance, \(r_e'\)

The signal meets a second, smaller resistance on its way out, and this one is inside the transistor.

The base-emitter junction is a diode, and a diode's current climbs very steeply with its voltage. Roughly every extra 60 mV multiplies the current by 10. So a small wiggle in \(V_{BE}\) makes a wiggle in \(I_E\), and the ratio of the two wiggles acts like a resistance. It's called \(r_e'\), the AC emitter resistance, written with a prime.

Its size depends on where the diode is sitting. The more DC current flows, the steeper the curve and the smaller \(r_e'\) gets. At room temperature it works out to

\[r_e' = \frac{25\text{ mV}}{I_E}\]

where \(I_E\) is the DC emitter current. The 25 mV is the thermal voltage, set by temperature. So finding \(r_e'\) always starts in the DC view, by finding \(I_E\).

Go deeper: where the 25 mV comes from

A diode follows \(I = I_S\,e^{V/V_T}\), where \(V_T = kT/q \approx 25\text{ mV}\) near room temperature. Its slope is \(dI/dV = I/V_T\), so a small wiggle sees a resistance of \(V_T/I\). That's \(r_e'\).

Watch out. The two names differ only by a prime. \(r_e'\), with the prime, is 25 mV/\(I_E\) and lives inside the transistor. \(r_e\), with no prime, is \(R_E \parallel R_L\) and lives outside it. Last week's lesson used the same names.

\(r_e'\) is set by the DC current. \(r_e\) is set by the resistors.

Topic 4 · what the source feels

Input impedance

The input impedance \(Z_{in}\) is the resistance a source sees when it looks into a circuit: the input voltage divided by the input current. Impedance is the word for resistance to AC.

It matters because every source has its own resistance inside, and that resistance forms a divider with \(Z_{in}\). A big \(Z_{in}\) draws almost no current and leaves almost all of the signal for the circuit. That's how the follower rescued the sensor in the demo at the top.

Work it out from the two rules. Wiggle the base by \(v_b\). The emitter follows, so the same wiggle sits across \(r_e'\) and \(r_e\) in series, and the signal current is \(i_e = v_b/(r_e' + r_e)\). The base supplies only \(i_b = i_e/\beta\) of that. Divide one by the other.

Impedance looking into the base\[Z_{in(base)} = \frac{v_b}{i_b} = \beta\,(r_e' + r_e)\]

From the base, every ohm in the emitter looks β times bigger. In Problem 1 the source connects straight to the base, with no bias resistors beside it, so \(Z_{in}\) is \(Z_{in(base)}\). The exact version uses β + 1, and the course formula uses β.

Analogy · a pulley block

Lift a heavy crate with a pulley block that has ten loops of rope. You pull ten times farther, but you only feel a tenth of the weight. From where you stand, the crate seems ten times lighter. The base feels the emitter's current divided by β, so the emitter's resistance looks β times bigger.

A follower makes its load look β times bigger to the source.

Topic 5 · voltage gain

Why the gain is a little under 1

The signal current \(i_e\) flows through \(r_e'\) and then \(r_e\). The base wiggle is \(i_e(r_e' + r_e)\), but the output is taken below \(r_e'\), so it only gets \(i_e\,r_e\). The two resistances split the wiggle like a voltage divider.

Emitter follower gain\[A_V = \frac{v_{out}}{v_{in}} = \frac{r_e}{r_e + r_e'}\]

\(r_e'\) is usually a few ohms and \(r_e\) is usually hundreds, so the gain lands around 0.9 to 0.99. The output rises when the input rises, so the gain is positive and the wave isn't flipped.

Two things push the gain closer to 1. A lighter load makes \(r_e\) bigger, and more bias current makes \(r_e'\) smaller. Try both below.

Analogy · a slightly springy tow rope

A truck tows a car on a rope that stretches a little. The car follows the truck, but the rope stretches whenever the car drags. The harder the car drags, the more the rope stretches and the more the car lags behind. \(r_e'\) is the stretch in the rope, and a heavy load is a car with its brakes on.

Where the base wiggle goespractice values · RE = 1 kΩ · β = 100

Slide the load down toward 22 Ω and the red sliver grows, because a heavy load shrinks \(r_e\) and drags the gain down. Slide the current up and \(r_e'\) shrinks, so more of the wiggle gets through. \(Z_{in}\) moves too: a heavier load is harder to drive, even through a follower.

\(A_V = r_e/(r_e + r_e')\): the outside resistance's share of the total.

Problem 2 · 8 pts

An amplifier with a follower on its output

The big idea

Last week's common-emitter amplifier has plenty of voltage gain but can't drive a heavy load. Put a follower after it, and each stage does the job it's good at.

Topic 6 · why we care

Why an amplifier needs a follower

Recall last week's common-emitter (CE) stage, which takes its output from the collector. Its gain is \(-r_c/r_e'\), where \(r_c\) is everything the collector sees to AC ground: the collector resistor in parallel with whatever is connected to the output.

That's the catch. Connect a heavy load to the collector and it lands in parallel with the collector resistor, so \(r_c\) collapses and the gain goes with it. In this homework's circuit, Q1 with nothing attached would have a gain of about −164. Hang the 150 Ω load straight on its collector and \(r_c\) drops to about 144 Ω, so the gain falls to about −6.

Now put a follower in between. Q1's collector sees the follower's input impedance, \(\beta(r_e' + r_e)\), which is tens of kilohms, so Q1 hardly notices it. The follower then drives the 150 Ω load with a gain just under 1, and most of the big gain survives.

Analogy · a singer and a sound system

A trained singer shapes the melody, but can't fill a stadium alone. A sound system adds no melody of its own. It copies the singer and supplies the power. The CE stage is the singer, and the follower is the sound system.

Give a CE stage a heavy load

A practice CE stage with \(R_C\) = 4.7 kΩ, \(r_e'\) = 20 Ω and its emitter fully bypassed. With nothing attached its gain is −235. A 100 Ω speaker on the collector drags that down to about −5. With a follower in between, Q1 sees about 14 kΩ instead of 100 Ω, and the speaker gets a gain of about −168. The speaker always connects through a coupling capacitor C. Without it, the speaker's path to ground would pull the DC bias around, and on the collector it would drag Q1 into saturation. All three waves after the input share one scale.

Use a CE stage for voltage gain and a follower to drive the load.

Topic 7 · the second stage

Q2's two resistances

Problem 2 gives you the first stage's results, so all the new work is on Q2. Q2's base connects to Q1's collector with a plain wire, with no capacitor in between. That's called direct coupling. Q2's base sits at whatever DC voltage Q1's collector has, \(V_{C1}\) = 5.9 V, and the amplified signal arrives on the same wire.

From there it's Problem 1 again. In the AC view, Q2's emitter has two paths to ground, RE2 and RL through C2, so \(r_{e2} = R_{E2} \parallel R_L\). In the DC view, C2 is open, the emitter sits 0.7 V below the base, all of \(I_{E2}\) goes down RE2, and \(r_{e2}' = 25\text{ mV}/I_{E2}\).

Go deeper: does the −144 already include Q2?

Yes. Q1's gain depends on everything its collector sees, and that includes Q2's input impedance. The homework gives −144 with Q2 attached, so use it as it is. If you work Q1 out yourself, its collector lands at 5.89 V, which matches the given 5.9 V.

With direct coupling, Q1's collector voltage is Q2's base bias.

Topic 8 · stages in a row

Gains in a row multiply

Q2 is a follower, so its gain is the same divider as in Problem 1: \(A_{V,CC} = r_{e2}/(r_{e2} + r_{e2}')\).

For the whole amplifier, follow the signal. Q1 multiplies the input by \(A_{V,CE}\) to make \(v_{c1}\). Q2 multiplies \(v_{c1}\) by \(A_{V,CC}\) to make the output. Chain the two ratios and \(v_{c1}\) cancels.

Cascaded gain\[A_{V,total} = \frac{v_{c1}}{v_{in}}\cdot\frac{v_{out}}{v_{c1}} = A_{V,CE}\,A_{V,CC}\]

Signs multiply too. The CE stage flips the wave, which is where its minus sign comes from, and the follower doesn't flip it back. One flip in the chain makes the total negative.

Analogy · changing money twice

Change dollars to euros, then euros to yen. Multiply the two exchange rates and you get dollars to yen, and the euros drop out. Stage gains chain the same way, and the voltage between the stages drops out.

The total gain is the product of the stage gains, and each inverting stage adds a minus sign.

Problem 3 · 2 pts

The Darlington pair

The big idea

Feed one follower's emitter into another's base. Their current gains multiply, so a tiny input current can control amps.

Topic 9 · why we care

Two transistors acting as one

Some loads need more current than one transistor can provide from a weak source. A small motor that draws 2 A, driven through a transistor with β = 100, needs 20 mA at the base. That's right at the limit of a typical microcontroller pin.

The fix is the Darlington pair. Q1's emitter feeds Q2's base directly, and both collectors go to the supply. Every bit of Q1's emitter current becomes Q2's base current. Each transistor multiplies the current by its β, so the pair multiplies it by about \(\beta_1\beta_2\).

\[I_{E2} \approx \beta_2\,I_{B2} = \beta_2\,I_{E1} \approx \beta_2\,\beta_1\,I_{B1} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad I_{B1} \approx \frac{I_{E2}}{\beta_1\,\beta_2}\]

With β = 100 each, that's a current gain of 10,000. The 2 A motor now needs only 0.2 mA at the input.

There's a price. The path from input to output crosses two base-emitter junctions, so the output sits 1.4 V below the input instead of 0.7 V. So you walk forward for voltages, taking off 0.7 V twice, and backward for currents, dividing by β twice.

Analogy · a pilot valve

Big hydraulic machines don't move their main valve by hand. A small lever moves a small pilot valve, which uses fluid pressure to move the big valve, which steers the main flow. Each stage multiplies the force. Q1 is the pilot valve and Q2 is the main valve.

How much current does the input need?practice values

The bars sit on a log scale, so each grid step is ten times the last. The dashed line is about the most a typical microcontroller pin can supply. With one transistor, a big load pushes the base current past it. Switch to the Darlington and the input current drops by another factor of β.

A Darlington multiplies the current gain by another β and costs a second 0.7 V.

Quick check: a Darlington with β = 50 in each transistor drives a 1 A load. About how much input current does it need?

1 A / (50 × 50) = 0.4 mA.

Problem 4 · 7 pts

A voltage regulator

The big idea

An op-amp decides what the output voltage should be, and a Darlington supplies the current. Feedback holds the output steady whatever the load does.

Topic 10 · what a regulator does

Brains and muscle

Almost every circuit needs a steady supply voltage, even while its current demand jumps around. A voltage regulator makes one from a rougher, higher supply, which here is 12 V.

Problem 4's regulator has two parts. The op-amp U1 is the brain. It compares a sample of the output with a steady 4.0 V reference and decides how hard to drive. The Darlington is the muscle. It carries the load current from the 12 V supply, so U1 only has to supply a tiny base current.

An op-amp has two inputs, + and −. It amplifies the difference between them, \(V_+ - V_-\), by a huge factor, often 100,000 or more, and its inputs draw almost no current.

Here the output is wired back to the − input through R1 and R2. If the output sags, \(V_-\) drops below \(V_+\), and U1 drives Q1's base harder, which lifts the output. If the output rises too far, U1 backs off. That correcting loop is negative feedback. It settles where the two inputs match, because any difference would be amplified into a correction.

Analogy · cruise control

You set 65 mph. The controller watches the speedometer, presses the gas harder uphill and eases off downhill. The engine does the work, and the controller only decides how much. The reference is the set speed, the divider is the speedometer, U1 is the controller, and the Darlington and 12 V supply are the engine.

Check yourself. The load suddenly draws more current and the output dips a little. What does U1 do?

Negative feedback drives U1 until \(V_- = V_+\).

Topic 11 · the output voltage

Setting the output voltage

Once feedback has settled, \(V_- = V_+ = V_{ref}\). R1 and R2 form a voltage divider from the output to ground, and the − input sits at their junction. No current goes into the − input, so the same current flows through both resistors, and the junction sits at R2's share of the output.

Regulator output\[V_- = V_{out}\,\frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2} = V_{ref} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad V_{out} = V_{ref}\left(1 + \frac{R_1}{R_2}\right)\]

The two 0.7 V drops don't appear in that formula. The feedback loop measures the output itself, after both junctions, so U1's output rises to 1.4 V above \(V_{out}\) to cover them.

That only works while U1 has room. Its output can't go above its own 12 V supply, and real op-amps usually stop a volt or so short. If \(V_{out}\) + 1.4 V would need more than that, the loop loses control.

Watch out. Don't subtract 1.4 V from \(V_{out}\). Feedback senses the output after both junctions, and U1 raises its own output to make up for them.
A regulator you can load down

A practice regulator with R2 = 10 kΩ and transistor gains of 50 and 100. Drag the load from 200 Ω down to 2 Ω. The output holds still while the load current climbs, and U1's current stays tiny. Then raise \(V_{ref}\) or R1 until U1 runs out of room.

Go deeper: where the wasted power goes

Q2 has the whole gap between 12 V and the output across it, with the full load current through it. In the practice regulator at its starting settings, that's (12 − 5) V × 0.25 A ≈ 1.75 W of heat in Q2, more than the 1.25 W the load gets. That's why linear regulators need heat sinks, and why phone chargers use switching regulators instead.

\(V_{out} = V_{ref}(1 + R_1/R_2)\), as long as U1 has room above it.

Topic 12 · why the transistors are there

Following the current back to the op-amp

Two currents leave Q2's emitter: the load current, and the small current down the R1 and R2 divider. Add them to get \(I_{E2}\).

Then walk backward through the Darlington, as in Problem 3. Q2's base needs \(I_{E2}/\beta_2\), which comes from Q1's emitter. Q1's base needs that divided by \(\beta_1\), and Q1's base current is exactly what flows out of U1's output pin.

\[I_{out,U1} = I_{B1} \approx \frac{I_{E2}}{\beta_1\,\beta_2} = \frac{I_{Rload} + I_{div}}{\beta_1\,\beta_2}\]

This is the payoff of the whole design. Most op-amps can only supply a few tens of milliamps. With the Darlington in place, U1 supplies microamps while the load draws most of an amp.

The load's current divided by \(\beta_1\beta_2\) is all the op-amp has to supply.

Transistor rules

β or β + 1?

The course uses β. The exact answer uses β + 1. They differ by about 1% per transistor.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing up \(r_e\) and \(r_e'\). The primed one is the transistor's own 25 mV/\(I_E\). The other is \(R_E \parallel R_L\).
  • Putting RL into the DC current. C1 and C2 block DC, so \(I_E\) flows only through the emitter resistor.
  • Forgetting β in \(Z_{in}\). From the base, the emitter's resistance looks β times bigger.
  • Expecting voltage gain from a follower. Its gain is always a little under 1, and positive.
  • Adding stage gains. They multiply, signs included.
  • Taking off only one 0.7 V in a Darlington. There are two junctions in the path.
  • Taking 1.4 V off a regulator's output. Feedback measures the output after both junctions.
  • Forgetting that U1's output needs room. It sits 1.4 V above \(V_{out}\) and has to stay below its supply.
