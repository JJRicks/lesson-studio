Swamped Amplifiers and the Small-Signal Model

Last week's amplifier had a huge gain, but that gain wobbles with temperature and changes from one transistor to the next. This week leaves a small resistor in the emitter's AC path to trade some gain for steadiness. Then the transistor gets replaced by a two-part model you can build in LTspice. Every idea is built from scratch, and both problems are worked step by step with each final answer hidden until you click.

Start here · no prior knowledge needed

What's new this week

An amplifier makes a small wiggling voltage bigger. It runs on two kinds of voltage at once.

  • DC (direct current) holds steady, like a 16 V supply. It sets the transistor partly on. That setup is called biasing.
  • AC (alternating current) wiggles up and down. It's the signal, riding on top of the DC.

Last week's amplifier put a capacitor across the whole emitter resistor. For AC, that left only the transistor's own tiny internal resistance, called \(r_e'\), in the emitter. The gain was \(-r_c/r_e'\), which came out in the hundreds. It had three problems.

  • The gain drifts. \(r_e'\) depends on the DC current and on temperature. Warm the transistor and the gain moves with it.
  • Big signals distort. \(r_e'\) isn't a fixed resistor. It changes during each swing, so the top and bottom of the output wave stop matching.
  • The input impedance is low. The amplifier draws a lot of current from whatever feeds it, which you'll measure in Problem 1d.

This homework's fix is to leave a small resistor in the emitter's AC path. It's called ac emitter feedback, or swamping, and Problem 1 analyzes it. Problem 2 then swaps the transistor for a simple model made of a resistor and a controlled current source, and builds it in LTspice.

Analogy · a wobbly table leg

A table standing on one short, wobbly leg tilts every time the leg shifts. Bolt a solid block under that leg and the same wobble barely moves the table. \(r_e'\) is the wobbly leg, and the extra emitter resistor is the block. The table sits a little lower, which is the gain you give up.

A little unbypassed emitter resistance trades gain for steadiness.

Problem 1 · 10 pts · four parts

The amplifier with ac emitter feedback

The big idea

The emitter resistance is split in two, and C3 bypasses only the bottom part. DC flows through both parts, so the bias works like a normal VDB amplifier. AC flows through the top part only, and that resistor adds to \(r_e'\) everywhere \(r_e'\) shows up: in the gain and in the input impedance.

Topic 1 · the DC pass

DC sees both resistors

Analyze an amplifier twice: once for DC to find the steady current, and once for AC to find what happens to the signal. Each pass uses its own version of the circuit.

PartIn the DC circuitIn the AC circuit
CapacitorsOpen (remove them)Short (replace with a wire)
\(V_{cc}\)Its full voltageGround
\(r_e\) (top emitter resistor)In the circuitIn the circuit
R4 (bottom emitter resistor)In the circuitShorted by C3

A capacitor blocks DC because current can't cross the gap between its plates. So at DC, C3 might as well not be there. The emitter current comes out of the transistor, goes down through \(r_e\), then down through R4. The two are in series, one after the other, so their resistances add.

The homework says to use simplified analysis. That means:

  • The base-emitter junction drops 0.7 V.
  • The divider is stiff, so the base current is too small to disturb it.
  • The collector current equals the emitter current, \(I_C \approx I_E\).
  • The thermal voltage, which you'll meet in the next topic, is 25 mV.
DC analysis with two emitter resistors\[V_B = V_{cc}\,\frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2} \qquad V_E = V_B - 0.7\text{ V} \qquad I_E = \frac{V_E}{r_e + R_4}\]

The stiff test from last week still applies. Compare R2 with β times the whole DC emitter resistance:

Stiff divider test\[R_2 \le 0.01\,\beta\,(r_e + R_4)\]
Analogy · two speed bumps and a side lane

The road out of the emitter has two speed bumps in a row. Steady traffic (DC) has to cross both. A side lane (C3) runs around only the second bump, and it only opens for traffic that keeps reversing direction (AC). So AC crosses the first bump and skips the second.

Which resistors carry which current

An example circuit with \(V_{cc}\) = 12 V, R1 = 8.2 kΩ and R2 = 1.5 kΩ. In the DC view, the dots run through both emitter resistors. In the AC view the capacitors turn into wires, and the signal goes through \(r_e\) and then around R4 through C3. Change R4 and only the DC numbers move.

Watch out. Use \(r_e + R_4\) for the DC current, not R4 alone. C3 doesn't carry DC, so it can't shorten the DC path.

DC sees \(r_e + R_4\). AC sees only \(r_e\).

Topic 2 · finding re′

AC emitter resistance \(r_e'\)

The base-emitter junction is a diode, a one-way valve for current. Its current grows exponentially with its voltage:

\[I = I_S\, e^{V/V_T}\]

\(V_T\) is the thermal voltage, about 25 mV near room temperature. \(I_S\) is a tiny constant set by the transistor.

A small signal only moves the junction a short way along this curve. Over a short stretch, the curve looks like a straight line, and a straight line on a current-voltage graph behaves like a resistor. That resistor is \(r_e'\), read "r-e-prime". Its value is the inverse of the curve's slope. The derivative is \(dI/dV = I/V_T\), and flipping it over gives the rule.

AC emitter resistance\[r_e' = \frac{25\text{ mV}}{I_E}\]

More DC current puts you higher up the curve where it's steeper, so \(r_e'\) gets smaller. This is how the DC pass feeds the AC pass. Some books use 26 mV. This homework's hint works with either, and the course uses 25 mV.

Two different r's. \(r_e'\), with the prime, lives inside the transistor and is set by the DC current. \(r_e\), with no prime, is the real 120 Ω resistor drawn in this homework's schematic. Some books call the real one \(R_{E1}\) to avoid the mix-up. Keep the prime and the two stay separate.

The diode curve, up close · live

Drag the current up. The dot climbs to where the curve is steeper, so the same small voltage wiggle makes a bigger current wiggle. The dashed tangent's steepness is \(1/r_e'\).

\(r_e'\) is set by the DC current through the emitter, so find \(I_E\) first.

Problem 1athe AC emitter resistance4 pts

Calculate \(r_e'\) for the voltage divider bias amplifier with ac emitter feedback, assuming β = 300 and simplified analysis. \(V_{cc}\) = 16 V, R1 = 3.3 kΩ, R2 = 560 Ω, R3 = 2.7 kΩ, \(r_e\) = 120 Ω (not bypassed), R4 = 560 Ω (bypassed by C3), Rl = 100 kΩ, Q1 = 2N3904.

  1. Draw the DC circuit and check the divider

    The capacitors are open at DC, so C1, C2, C3, the signal and Rl drop out. The divider is stiff if R2 is at most \(0.01\,\beta\,(r_e + R_4)\):

    \[0.01\,(300)\,(120\ \Omega + 560\ \Omega) = 2040\ \Omega\]

    R2 is 560 Ω, which is smaller, so it's stiff.

  2. Find the base voltage

    Ignore the base current, so the same current runs down through R1 and R2. Compute it.

    \[V_B = 16\text{ V}\;\frac{560\ \Omega}{3300\ \Omega + 560\ \Omega}\]
  3. Drop one diode voltage

    The emitter sits 0.7 V below the base: \(V_E = V_B - 0.7\text{ V}\).

  4. Send the current through both emitter resistors

    C3 is open at DC, so the emitter current goes through \(r_e\) and R4 in series. Compute it.

    \[I_E = \frac{V_E}{r_e + R_4} = \frac{V_E}{680\ \Omega}\]
  5. Turn the current into \(r_e'\)

    Use your \(I_E\) and compute it.

    \[r_e' = \frac{25\text{ mV}}{I_E}\]
Reveal the answer
re′ = 10.49 Ω10.486 Ω to five figures.

The chain: \(V_B\) = 16 × 560/3860 = 2.321 V, \(V_E\) = 1.621 V, \(I_E\) = 1.621 V / 680 Ω = 2.384 mA, and 25 mV / 2.384 mA = 10.49 Ω.

Check: the collector sits at 16 − (2.384 mA)(2.7 kΩ) = 9.56 V, about 7.9 V above the emitter, so the transistor is active. Including the base current moves \(I_E\) by only 0.23%.

Topic 3 · finding rc

AC collector resistance \(r_c\)

Now switch to the AC circuit. The output voltage forms at the collector, so ask what resistance the AC collector current runs into. It's written with a lowercase \(r_c\), because lowercase means an AC quantity.

Follow the AC rules. C2 becomes a wire, so Rl connects straight to the collector. \(V_{cc}\) becomes ground, because a supply that never wiggles is the same as ground to a signal. So R3 also runs from the collector to ground. Two resistors from the same point to ground are in parallel.

AC collector resistance\[r_c = R_C \parallel R_L = \frac{R_C R_L}{R_C + R_L}\]

The symbol \(\parallel\) means "in parallel with". In this homework, \(R_C\) is R3 and \(R_L\) is Rl. Nothing about the emitter changes this, so it works exactly like last week.

The load joins in for AC

An example circuit with a 2.2 kΩ collector resistor. Make the load small and \(r_c\) drops with it. Make it huge and \(r_c\) settles just under the collector resistor.

AC sees the collector resistor and the load side by side.

Topic 4 · finding AV

Gain with emitter feedback

Voltage gain \(A_V\) is how many times bigger the output wave is than the input wave. Follow the signal through the AC circuit.

  1. The input reaches the base

    C1 is a short for AC, so \(v_b = v_{in}\). Lowercase \(v\) and \(i\) mean the AC part.

  2. The emitter path now has two resistances

    C3 shorts R4 to ground, but \(r_e\) sits above it with no capacitor. So the signal current leaving the emitter goes through \(r_e'\) inside the transistor, then through \(r_e\), then through C3.

  3. Ohm's law on the emitter path

    The whole input lands across those two in series: \(i_e = v_{in}/(r_e' + r_e)\).

  4. Ohm's law on the collector

    Since \(i_c \approx i_e\), the same current runs through \(r_c\). More current means a bigger drop, which pulls the collector down: \(v_{out} = -i_e\,r_c\).

Divide the output by the input, and \(i_e\) cancels:

Gain with an unbypassed emitter resistor\[A_V = -\frac{r_c}{r_e' + r_e} \;\approx\; -\frac{r_c}{r_e} \quad\text{when } r_e \gg r_e'\]

The minus sign means the output is upside down compared with the input.

Why it's called feedback. When the input pushes more current through the transistor, that current also flows through \(r_e\) and lifts the emitter. A higher emitter cancels part of the push at the base. Part of the output's effect is fed back to fight the input. Engineers call that negative feedback, and it always trades gain for steadiness.

Why it's called swamping. Once \(r_e\) is several times larger than \(r_e'\), the sum \(r_e' + r_e\) barely notices when \(r_e'\) changes. The wobbly part is swamped, which means drowned out, by the steady part. The gain is then set mostly by two ordinary resistors, \(r_c\) and \(r_e\), which don't drift.

Analogy · weighing a cat

Put a squirming cat on a bathroom scale by itself and the reading jumps all over. Weigh yourself holding the cat and the squirming barely moves the number. \(r_e'\) is the cat. \(r_e\) is you, a big steady weight that keeps the total calm.

How much the gain drifts

An example with \(r_c\) = 2.2 kΩ. The shaded band is a realistic spread of \(r_e'\), from a hot, high-current transistor on the left to a cool, low-current one on the right. Without \(r_e\) (dashed) the gain swings across the band. Raise \(r_e\) and the solid curve flattens.

Going further: why the wave also gets cleaner

\(r_e'\) isn't truly constant during a swing. On the up swing the current rises, so \(r_e'\) shrinks and the gain rises. On the down swing it's the opposite. Without \(r_e\), a large input comes out with one half taller than the other. With \(r_e\) in the path, those changes are a small slice of \(r_e' + r_e\), so both halves get nearly the same gain. The hero at the top shows this when you slide \(r_e\) to zero with a big input.

Leave \(r_e\) in the AC path and it adds to \(r_e'\) on the bottom of the gain.

Topic 5 · finding Zin

Input impedance

Impedance is resistance to AC. The input impedance \(Z_{in}\) is the resistance the signal source sees when it looks into the amplifier: the input voltage divided by the input current.

\[Z_{in} = \frac{v_{in}}{i_{in}}\]

It matters because real sources have some resistance of their own. That resistance and \(Z_{in}\) form a voltage divider. A low \(Z_{in}\) takes a big share of the current and leaves less signal for the amplifier. Higher is better.

At the base node, the input current has three ways to go:

  • Up through R1. \(V_{cc}\) is AC ground, so R1 goes to ground.
  • Down through R2 to ground.
  • Into the base of the transistor.

The first two are plain resistors. The third needs a closer look.

Looking into the base

  1. The base voltage

    The base sits on top of the emitter path, so \(v_b = i_e\,(r_e' + r_e)\).

  2. The base current

    The base only supplies a small slice of the emitter current: \(i_b = i_e/\beta\).

  3. Divide

    \(Z_{in(base)} = v_b / i_b\). The \(i_e\) cancels and β moves to the top.

Impedance looking into the base\[Z_{in(base)} = \beta\,(r_e' + r_e)\]

From the base, every emitter resistance looks β times bigger. That's where swamping helps a lot. Without \(r_e\) the base would show only \(\beta r_e'\), which is small.

Analogy · a gearbox

Turning a heavy wheel through a gearbox feels easy, because the gears multiply your effort. The base current is your hand, and β is the gear ratio. The emitter resistance is the heavy wheel, and from the base side it feels β times harder to move. That's what a high impedance means.

The three paths sit side by side between the base node and ground, so they combine in parallel:

Amplifier input impedance\[Z_{in} = R_1 \parallel R_2 \parallel \beta\,(r_e' + r_e) = \frac{1}{\dfrac{1}{R_1} + \dfrac{1}{R_2} + \dfrac{1}{\beta(r_e' + r_e)}}\]
Three ways into the amplifier · live

An example with R1 = 8.2 kΩ, R2 = 1.5 kΩ and \(r_e'\) = 12 Ω. Dot speed shows how much of the input current takes each path. With \(r_e\) at zero, the base takes a big share and \(Z_{in}\) drops. Raise \(r_e\) and the base path slows to a trickle, leaving the divider to set \(Z_{in}\).

Parallel resistances combine to less than the smallest one, so the smallest path sets \(Z_{in}\).

Problem 1dthe input impedance2 pts

Calculate \(Z_{in}\) for the same circuit. The hint says it should be between 460 and 480 Ω.

  1. Stand at the input

    \(Z_{in}\) is what vin sees through C1, which is a short for AC. \(V_{cc}\) is AC ground, so R1 and R2 both run from the base node to ground.

  2. Look into the base

    The emitter's AC resistance is \(r_e' + r_e\), and the base sees it β times bigger. Use your \(r_e'\) from part a.

Problem 2 · 12 pts · four parts

The small-signal π model in LTspice

The big idea

For small signals, a transistor acts like two simple parts: a resistor \(\beta r_e'\) from base to emitter, and a current source from collector to emitter that copies the base current, times β. Build those two parts in LTspice, feed in a small sine wave, and the plot shows the gain.

Topic 6 · replacing the transistor

The small-signal model

A small-signal model describes only the wiggles. The DC pass has already done its job: it set the current, and the current set \(r_e'\). After that, the DC levels are steady, so the model leaves them out. Every DC source becomes zero, just like in the AC circuit.

What's left of the transistor comes down to two facts you've already used.

  • Base to emitter is a resistor. Looking into the base, the junction's \(r_e'\) looks β times bigger, so the model puts a resistor of \(\beta r_e'\) between base and emitter. Many books call it \(r_\pi\).
  • Collector to emitter is a controlled current source. The collector current is β times the base current, \(i_c = \beta\,i_b\), whatever the collector voltage does. A source whose value is set by some other current or voltage in the circuit is called a dependent source. It's drawn as a diamond to tell it apart from an ordinary source.
Simplified small-signal π model\[\text{base to emitter: } \beta\,r_e' \qquad\qquad \text{collector to emitter: } i_c = \beta\,i_b\]

The name comes from the full version, which adds a few more parts and is drawn in the shape of the Greek letter π. The homework uses the simplified version with just these two.

Analogy · power steering

Your hands put a small force on the steering wheel, and a pump adds a much larger force to turn the wheels. The pump's force follows your hands and ignores everything else. The base current is your hands. The dependent source is the pump, always β times stronger.

From transistor to model · live
base current \(i_b\)collector current \(i_c = \beta i_b\)

A 5 mV wiggle drives the base. Switch between the transistor and its model. The terminals stay put and the currents don't change, because the model is a stand-in that behaves the same for small signals. Raise β and the base dots slow down while the collector dots stay the same, because the collector current depends on IE. Raise IE and both speed up.

Quick check: in Problem 1, what would the base-to-emitter resistor be?

\(\beta\,(r_e' + r_e)\). The external \(r_e\) sits in the emitter path too, so the base sees it multiplied by β as well. That's the \(Z_{in(base)}\) from Problem 1d.

For small signals, a transistor is a resistor \(\beta r_e'\) on the input and a β-times copy of the base current on the output.

Topic 7 · the simulator

Building the model in LTspice

LTspice has no part called "dependent current source", but it has something more flexible. The bi component, an Arbitrary Behavioral Current Source, makes whatever current you type as a formula, and the formula can mention other currents in the circuit. \(I(\text{Rin})\) means "the current through Rin right now". So this line makes a current 100 times Rin's current:

I={100*I(Rin)}

The plain current component can't do this. Its value is fixed, or follows a waveform you set in advance, so it can't follow another current. That's why the homework insists on bi.

Rin's current plays the part of \(i_b\). That makes 1 kΩ equal to \(\beta r_e'\), so this model stands for a transistor with \(r_e'\) = 10 Ω.

Which way is positive?

The sign of the gain depends on two LTspice conventions, so it pays to know them.

  • \(I(\text{R})\) is positive into pin 1. On a resistor placed upright without rotating, pin 1 is the top. Rotate it 180° and pin 1 is at the bottom, so every value of \(I(\text{Rin})\) changes sign.
  • A current source pushes current along its arrow. B1's arrow points down, so it pulls current out of its top node and sends it down to ground.

Put them together in Figure 2. A positive vin pushes current down through Rin, so \(I(\text{Rin})\) is positive. B1 then pulls 100 times that out of the Vout node. That current has to come up through rc from ground, so Vout ends up below ground. The output is upside down, just like a real common-emitter amplifier.

Watch out. If Rin or B1 ends up rotated 180°, the gain comes out positive. The circuit is the same, but LTspice is counting current the other way. Match Figure 2's orientation.

Going further: the LTspice clicks
  • Open the parts library with F2. The signal source is in the Misc folder. Type bi to find the behavioral current source, and res for resistors.
  • Ctrl+R rotates a part while you place it. Twice is 180°, which flips the current sign.
  • G places a ground. F4 places a net label: name the nodes Vin and Vout so the plot names match.
  • T places text, which is how your name goes on the schematic.
  • Right-click a part to set its value. For B1, the value is the whole formula, I={100*I(Rin)}.
  • Simulate → Edit Simulation Cmd (Configure Analysis in newer versions) sets up the transient run.
  • LTspice reads both m and M as milli. Mega is Meg. So 1m and 1mV both mean 1 millivolt.

bi lets one current follow another, which is exactly what \(i_c = \beta i_b\) needs. Orientation decides the sign.

Topic 8 · from a plot to a number

Reading gain off a plot

Gain is the output divided by the input. On a plot, you read both at the same instant.

  1. Pick a moment

    A peak is easiest, because the wave is flat there and small timing errors don't matter.

  2. Read both traces at that moment

    Read vin and vout at the same time, including their signs.

  3. Divide

    \(A_V = v_{out}/v_{in}\). If one is positive and the other negative, the gain is negative, which means the amplifier inverts.

You can also divide the peak-to-peak heights, then add a minus sign if the waves move in opposite directions. Watch the units: if vin is in millivolts and vout in volts, convert one first.

In LTspice, click a trace's name above its pane to attach a cursor. Drag the cursor along the trace and a small window shows the time and the value.

Read the gain · practice

Drag across the plot to move the cursor. Put it on a peak, read both values, divide, and decide the sign. Then check. Each new amplifier has a different gain, and some don't invert.

Topic 9 · the gain formula

What sets the gain

The model is simple enough to solve by hand. Follow the signal around it.

  1. The input current

    vin sits right across Rin, so \(i_b = v_{in}/R_{in}\).

  2. The copied current

    B1 makes \(i_c = \beta\,i_b\).

  3. The output voltage

    That current is pulled out of the Vout node, and it comes up through rc from ground: \(v_{out} = -i_c\,r_c\).

Gain of the small-signal model\[A_V = \frac{v_{out}}{v_{in}} = -\frac{\beta\,r_c}{R_{in}}\]
ChangeEffect on the gain's size \(|A_V|\)Why
Raise βGoes upMore output current for the same input current
Raise \(R_{in}\)Goes downLess input current for the same input voltage
Raise \(r_c\)Goes upThe same output current makes a bigger voltage

In a real amplifier each piece has a meaning. \(R_{in}\) is \(\beta r_e'\), so β cancels and the gain is \(-r_c/r_e'\), the same formula as last week. \(r_c\) is the collector resistor in parallel with the load, \(R_C \parallel R_L\).

Quick check: double \(R_{in}\) and leave the rest alone. What happens to the gain?

Its size halves. Half the input current flows, so B1 copies half as much.

Gain is β times the output resistance over the input resistance, with a minus sign for the flip.

  3. See what's left

Cheat sheet

DC pass
\(V_B = V_{cc}\dfrac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}\)
\(V_E = V_B - 0.7\)
\(I_E = \dfrac{V_E}{r_e + R_4}\)

Caps open, so DC sees both emitter resistors.

AC resistances
\(r_e' = 25\text{ mV}/I_E\)
\(r_c = R_C \parallel R_L\)

Caps short, \(V_{cc}\) = ground. C3 removes R4, not \(r_e\).

Gain with emitter feedback
\(A_V = -\dfrac{r_c}{r_e' + r_e}\)

About \(-r_c/r_e\) when \(r_e \gg r_e'\). Steadier, smaller.

Input impedance
\(Z_{in(base)} = \beta(r_e' + r_e)\)
\(Z_{in} = R_1 \parallel R_2 \parallel Z_{in(base)}\)

Parallel: add \(1/R\) values, then flip.

π model
\(b\) to \(e\): \(\beta r_e'\)
\(c\) to \(e\): \(i_c = \beta i_b\)

In LTspice: bi with I={β*I(Rin)}.

Model gain
\(A_V = -\dfrac{\beta\,r_c}{R_{in}}\)

Read from a plot: vout ÷ vin at the same instant, sign included.

Common mistakes

  • Using R4 alone for the DC emitter current. C3 is open at DC, so it's \(r_e + R_4\).
  • Mixing up \(r_e'\), inside the transistor, with \(r_e\), the 120 Ω resistor.
  • Leaving \(r_e\) out of the gain, or putting R4 into it. C3 shorts R4 only.
  • Forgetting β when looking into the base. It's \(\beta(r_e' + r_e)\), not \(r_e' + r_e\).
  • Forgetting R1 and R2 in \(Z_{in}\), or adding them in series. For AC they're both to ground, in parallel.
  • Using LTspice's current source instead of bi. Only bi can follow another current.
  • Rotating Rin or B1 by 180°, which flips the sign of the gain.
  • Typing 1M and expecting mega. LTspice reads M as milli. Mega is Meg.
  • Dropping the minus sign on \(A_V\). It's part of the answer.
