Week 12 · MOSFET HW 2 · 16 pts

MOSFETs as Switches

Last week you measured two MOSFETs in LTspice. This week you put them to work as switches, the job most MOSFETs in the world are doing right now. A switch that's on should pass current while keeping almost no voltage for itself, and a MOSFET comes close but not all the way. You'll take a datasheet's numbers, turn them into a model with Sah's equations, predict the current through an n-channel and a p-channel switch, and find how much resistance each one keeps when it's on.

A MOSFET switch and its load · live

The n-channel switch starts fully on, with Vin at 5 V. Its curve shoots up so steeply that it meets the load line at only a few hundred millivolts, so nearly all of the 5 V reaches the load. Slide Vin down and the curve sags. The crossing slides right along the load line, M1 keeps more of the voltage and heats up, and below the threshold the current stops. The p-channel switch turns on the other way: Vin at 0 V. These demos use a practice part (VT = 1.5 V) and a 47 Ω load, so they don't show any answers.

Start here · no prior knowledge needed

The big picture: how good is the switch?

Most MOSFETs in the world are switches. A microcontroller pin turns an LED strip, a motor or a heater on and off by driving a MOSFET's gate, and the MOSFET carries the load's current straight from the power supply. The pin supplies no steady current, which is the gift of the insulated gate from last week.

A perfect switch would pass any current with zero volts across it. A real MOSFET keeps a small resistance when it's fully on, called its on-resistance, \(R_{DS(on)}\). That resistance costs twice. Some of the supply's voltage stays across the switch instead of reaching the load, and the switch turns that power into heat, \(P = I^2 R_{DS(on)}\). Push 1 A through a 3 Ω switch and it gives off 3 W, enough to cook a small part. That's why the MOSFETs in a laptop charger are built for a few thousandths of an ohm.

This homework finds \(R_{DS(on)}\) the way a designer would, before buying anything, from the datasheet:

  • Problem 1a uses the datasheet's single test point and Sah's equations to find \(\beta_n\), the number that says how strong the n-channel part is.
  • Problem 1b uses \(\beta_n\) to predict the current when the part works as a low-side switch, between its load and ground.
  • Problem 1c turns that current into the switch's \(R_{DS(on)}\).
  • Problem 2 repeats all three for a p-channel part working as a high-side switch, between the supply and its load. It comes out with about twice the resistance, and you'll see why.
Analogy · a tollbooth with the barrier up

On a toll road, even with the barrier raised, every car slows a little as it squeezes through the booth. A fully-on MOSFET is the booth with its barrier up. \(R_{DS(on)}\) measures how much it slows things down, and the more traffic you send through, the more total delay it adds. In a circuit, the traffic is current and the delay is voltage lost.

\(R_{DS(on)}\) is the price of a MOSFET switch: the voltage it keeps and the heat it makes.

Problem 1 · an n-channel low-side switch · 8 pts

From a datasheet to an on-resistance

The big idea

Sah's equations turn a datasheet's threshold and one test point into a model that predicts the current anywhere. Use the test point to find \(\beta_n\), predict the current in the switch, and divide to get its on-resistance.

Topic 1a · from first principles

Sah's equations

What it is. Sah's equations are two formulas for a MOSFET's drain current, one for each region where it conducts. Chih-Tang Sah published them in 1964, and they're still the first model every engineer learns. They're also the model behind LTspice's simple MOSFETs: last week's Kp is this week's \(\beta\).

Why we care. With just two numbers, \(V_T\) and \(\beta\), they predict the current at any gate and drain voltages. That's what lets you check, on paper, whether a part will switch your load before you build anything.

Sah's equations · n-channel

Cutoff, \(V_{GS} \le V_T\): \(\;I_D = 0\)

Triode, \(V_{DS} \lt V_{GS} - V_T\):

\[I_D = \beta_n\left[(V_{GS} - V_T)\,V_{DS} - \tfrac{1}{2}V_{DS}^2\right]\]

Saturation, \(V_{DS} \ge V_{GS} - V_T\):

\[I_D = \frac{\beta_n}{2}\,(V_{GS} - V_T)^2\]

\(\beta_n\) has units of A/V², and it packs the part's construction into one number: \(\beta_n = \mu_n C_{ox} (W/L)\). Here \(\mu_n\) is how easily electrons move through silicon, \(C_{ox}\) is how much charge the gate pulls in per volt (a thinner oxide gives more), and \(W/L\) is the channel's width over its length. A wide, short channel under a thin oxide makes a big \(\beta_n\) and a strong part.

The two formulas meet exactly at the edge, \(V_{DS} = V_{GS} - V_T\). Each one is only true on its own side of it. Past the edge, the triode formula curves back down, which would mean more drain voltage pushing less current. That doesn't happen: the channel pinches off and the current levels out at the saturation value. Before the edge, the saturation formula would overstate the current. So the first step of every problem is the same. Compare \(V_{DS}\) with \(V_{GS} - V_T\), and only then pick the formula.

Analogy · maps of two countries

A road map of France is no use once you cross into Spain. Each of Sah's equations is a map of one region, and \(V_{GS} - V_T\) is the border. Check which side you're on before you read the map.

Two formulas, one curve · practice part

The blue zone is triode and the green zone is saturation. Slide the dot across the edge and watch the two formulas trade places. Inside triode the saturation formula is too big. Past the edge the triode formula (dashed red) falls away while the real current stays flat. The practice part has VT = 1.5 V and β = 0.18 A/V².

Watch out. Using the wrong formula is the most common mistake on this homework. Find the region first, every time.

Compare \(V_{DS}\) with \(V_{GS} - V_T\) first. Below it, use the triode formula. At or above it, use the square law.

Topic 1b · reading a datasheet

One test point pins β

What it is. Datasheets rarely print \(\beta\). They print \(V_T\) and one measured point: the drain current at a stated \(V_{GS}\) and \(V_{DS}\). The test usually uses a large \(V_{DS}\) so the part sits in saturation.

Why we care. Once you know \(V_T\), the square law has only one unknown, \(\beta\). One point is enough to find it, and with \(\beta\) in hand, Sah's equations can predict every other point, including the switch circuits in parts b and c.

The method has two steps. Confirm the point is in saturation, because the test's \(V_{DS}\) should be at least \(V_{GS} - V_T\). Then solve the square law for \(\beta\):

\[\beta_n = \frac{2\,I_D}{(V_{GS} - V_T)^2}\]
Fit β to a datasheet point · practice part

This practice datasheet says VT = 1.5 V and ID = 0.81 A at VGS = 4.5 V. Slide β until the curve passes through the ring. Only one value works, and the formula above lands on it directly.

Quick check: a datasheet's test uses \(V_{GS}\) = 4 V and \(V_{DS}\) = 0.5 V on a part with \(V_T\) = 2 V. Can you use the square law?

No. The edge is at 2 V and 0.5 V is below it, so that point is in triode. You'd solve the triode formula for β instead.

Problem 1aβn from the datasheet3 pts

Find \(\beta_n\) using Sah's equations. The datasheet gives \(V_T\) = 2.0 V, with \(I_D\) = 0.5 A at \(V_{GS}\) = 5.0 V and \(V_{DS}\) = 10 V.

  1. Read the test point

    \(V_{GS}\) = 5.0 V, \(V_{DS}\) = 10 V and \(I_D\) = 0.5 A, with \(V_T\) = 2.0 V.

  2. Find the region

    The edge is \(V_{GS} - V_T\) = 3 V. \(V_{DS}\) = 10 V is at least that, so the part is in saturation.

  3. Solve the square law for βn

    Compute it.

    \[\beta_n = \frac{2\,I_D}{(V_{GS} - V_T)^2} = \frac{2(0.5\text{ A})}{(3\text{ V})^2}\]
Reveal the answer
βn = 0.1111 A/V²Exactly 1/9 A/V², or 111.1 mA/V². Keep the fraction for parts b and c, so rounding doesn't creep in.
Topic 1c · the circuit

The low-side switch

What it is. Problem 1's circuit is a low-side switch. The load, R1, hangs from the 5 V supply down to the drain. The MOSFET's source goes to ground, and Vin drives the gate. The MOSFET sits on the low side of the load, between it and ground. Vin at 5 V turns it on and pulls Vout down near 0 V. Vin at 0 V turns it off, no current flows, and Vout rises to 5 V.

Why we care. This is the standard way a microcontroller drives anything bigger than an LED. The pin only has to set the gate voltage, and the load's current comes from the supply, through the load and the MOSFET, to ground.

The MOSFET and R1 carry the same current, so the circuit has to satisfy both of them at once. R1 allows \(I_D = (5\text{ V} - V_{DS})/R_1\), a straight line called the load line. The MOSFET allows its Sah curve. The circuit settles where the two cross, which is the dot in the demo at the top of the page. When the gate is driven hard, the MOSFET's curve is so steep that the crossing lands at a small \(V_{DS}\), deep in triode.

The homework gives you Vout = 0.2 V, so you don't need R1 at all. With the source grounded, \(V_{GS}\) is Vin and \(V_{DS}\) is Vout.

Go deeper: what R1 must be

R1 carries the same current with 5 V − 0.2 V = 4.8 V across it, so once you've found \(I_D\) in part b, R1 = 4.8 V / \(I_D\). Try it after you've revealed part b's answer. It's not asked, but it shows the load gets 96% of the supply.

Problem 1bthe switch's drain current3 pts

Find \(I_D\) in the low-side switch with Vin = 5.0 V, given that Vout = 0.2 V. Use Sah's equations and your \(\beta_n\).

  1. Read the voltages

    The source is grounded, so \(V_{GS}\) = Vin = 5.0 V and \(V_{DS}\) = Vout = 0.2 V.

  2. Find the region

    The edge is still \(V_{GS} - V_T\) = 3 V. \(V_{DS}\) = 0.2 V is below it, so the part is in triode.

  3. Use the triode formula

    Put in \(\beta_n\) from part a. Compute it.

    \[I_D = \beta_n\left[(3\text{ V})(0.2\text{ V}) - \tfrac{1}{2}(0.2\text{ V})^2\right]\]
Reveal the answer
ID = 64.44 mA(1/9)(0.6 − 0.02) = 0.06444 A. The square law would have said 0.5 A, almost eight times too much, which is why the region check matters.
Topic 1d · from first principles

On-resistance, RDS(on)

What it is. \(R_{DS(on)}\) is the resistance of a fully-on MOSFET: the voltage across it divided by the current through it, \(R_{DS(on)} = V_{DS}/I_D\).

Why we care. It sets the switch's losses. At current \(I\), the switch keeps \(I\,R_{DS(on)}\) of the supply's voltage and turns \(I^2 R_{DS(on)}\) into heat. Datasheets list it near the top of page one, and choosing a MOSFET for a power circuit often comes down to this one number.

Why does a MOSFET act like a resistor at all? When \(V_{DS}\) is small, the \(\tfrac12 V_{DS}^2\) term in the triode formula is tiny, so

\[I_D \approx \beta_n (V_{GS} - V_T)\,V_{DS} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad R_{DS(on)} \approx \frac{1}{\beta_n (V_{GS} - V_T)}\]

Current proportional to voltage is Ohm's law. The channel is a strip of conducting silicon, and the gate sets how thick it is. Drive the gate harder and the channel gets thicker, so the resistance drops. That's why datasheets list \(R_{DS(on)}\) at a stated \(V_{GS}\), often 10 V and 4.5 V. A logic-level MOSFET is one that's guaranteed to be fully on at 4.5 V or less, so a microcontroller pin can drive it.

Gate drive against on-resistance · practice part

Measured the way datasheets do it, at a small VDS of 0.1 V. Compare 3.3 V logic with 5 V logic and with a 10 V gate driver. Near the threshold the resistance shoots up, so a gate signal that barely clears VT makes a poor switch. The dashed estimate sits a little below the real curve, because it drops the ½VDS² term.

More gate drive means a thicker channel and a lower \(R_{DS(on)}\).

Problem 2 · a p-channel high-side switch · 8 pts

The same steps, mirrored

The big idea

A p-channel MOSFET follows the same equations once you measure from the source: \(V_{SG}\), \(V_{SD}\) and \(|V_T|\). As a high-side switch it connects a load to the supply. With the same size and the same 5 V of drive, it keeps about twice the resistance, because holes move more slowly than electrons.

Topic 2a · from first principles

Sah's equations for a p-channel part

What it is. A p-channel part's voltages and current are all negative when it's on: \(V_{GS}\), \(V_{DS}\), \(V_T\) and \(I_D\). Measure the voltages from the source the other way round, as \(V_{SG}\) and \(V_{SD}\), use \(|V_T|\) and \(|I_D|\), and every number is positive again. Then Sah's equations read exactly as they did for n-channel.

Why we care. You don't need a second set of formulas, only a habit of flipping signs at the start. The datasheet here writes its test point in negative numbers, which is the same test as \(V_{SG}\) = 5 V and \(V_{SD}\) = 10 V.

Sah's equations · p-channel

Triode, \(V_{SD} \lt V_{SG} - |V_T|\):

\[|I_D| = \beta_p\left[(V_{SG} - |V_T|)\,V_{SD} - \tfrac{1}{2}V_{SD}^2\right]\]

Saturation, \(V_{SD} \ge V_{SG} - |V_T|\):

\[|I_D| = \frac{\beta_p}{2}\,(V_{SG} - |V_T|)^2\]

\(\beta_p = \mu_p C_{ox}(W/L)\), with the mobility of holes in place of electrons. Holes move two to three times more slowly than electrons in silicon. So for the same size and oxide, \(\beta_p\) comes out two to three times smaller, and you'll see that in part a.

For p-channel, measure from the source: \(V_{SG}\), \(V_{SD}\), \(|V_T|\), \(|I_D|\). Then use the same equations.

Problem 2aβp from the datasheet3 pts

Find \(\beta_p\) using Sah's equations. The datasheet gives \(V_T\) = −2.0 V, with \(I_D\) = −0.24 A at \(V_{GS}\) = −5.0 V and \(V_{DS}\) = −10 V, which is the same as \(V_{SG}\) = 5.0 V and \(V_{SD}\) = 10 V.

  1. Flip the signs

    \(V_{SG}\) = 5.0 V, \(V_{SD}\) = 10 V, \(|V_T|\) = 2.0 V and \(|I_D|\) = 0.24 A.

  2. Find the region

    The edge is \(V_{SG} - |V_T|\) = 3 V, and \(V_{SD}\) = 10 V is past it: saturation.

  3. Solve the square law for βp

    Compute it.

    \[\beta_p = \frac{2\,|I_D|}{(V_{SG} - |V_T|)^2} = \frac{2(0.24\text{ A})}{(3\text{ V})^2}\]
Reveal the answer
βp = 0.05333 A/V²Exactly 4/75 A/V², or 53.33 mA/V². That's 48% of βn, the hole-mobility penalty in one number.
Topic 2b · the circuit

The high-side switch

What it is. Problem 2's circuit is a high-side switch. The p-channel MOSFET's source sits on the 5 V supply, its drain feeds the load R1, and R1 goes to ground. Vin at 0 V pulls the gate 5 V below the source, so \(V_{SG}\) = 5 V and the switch is on, pulling Vout up near 5 V. Vin at 5 V makes \(V_{SG}\) = 0 and turns it off.

Why we care. Some loads must stay connected to ground, like a sensor that shares its ground wire with the circuit reading it. To turn those off you have to cut their supply instead. A phone uses switches like this to power whole sections of its circuitry up and down. Notice the logic is upside down from Problem 1: a low gate signal turns this switch on. Flip the demo at the top of the page to p-channel to see it.

Measure everything from the source, which sits at 5 V:

  • \(V_{SG}\) = 5 V − Vin
  • \(V_{SD}\) = 5 V − Vout

With Vout = 4.8 V, the switch keeps \(V_{SD}\) = 0.2 V, the same small voltage as Problem 1. That makes a fair comparison between the two switches.

Problem 2bthe switch's drain current3 pts

Find \(I_D\) in the high-side switch with Vin = 0.0 V, given that Vout = 4.8 V. Use Sah's equations and your \(\beta_p\).

  1. Read the voltages from the source

    The source is at 5 V. \(V_{SG}\) = 5 V − 0 V = 5 V, and \(V_{SD}\) = 5 V − 4.8 V = 0.2 V.

  2. Find the region

    The edge is \(V_{SG} - |V_T|\) = 3 V, and 0.2 V is below it: triode.

  3. Use the triode formula, then add the sign

    Put in \(\beta_p\) from part a to get \(|I_D|\). The current flows out of a p-channel part's drain, so \(I_D\) is negative. Compute it.

    \[|I_D| = \beta_p\left[(3\text{ V})(0.2\text{ V}) - \tfrac{1}{2}(0.2\text{ V})^2\right]\]
Reveal the answer
ID = −30.93 mA|ID| = (4/75)(0.58) = 30.93 mA, flowing out of the drain and down through R1. That's under half of Problem 1's current at the same 0.2 V, because βp is under half of βn.
Topic 2c · why we care

Why the p-channel switch keeps more resistance

What it is. \(R_{DS(on)}\) for a p-channel part is \(V_{SD}/|I_D|\), and in triode it's close to \(1/(\beta_p(V_{SG} - |V_T|))\). With the same drive and the same \(V_T\), a smaller \(\beta\) means a bigger resistance, in exact proportion.

Why we care. To match an n-channel switch's resistance, a p-channel part has to be two to three times wider, which makes it bigger and more expensive. That's why designers use n-channel switches wherever the circuit allows, and why a CMOS chip's p-channel transistors are drawn wider than its n-channel ones. In fact, Problems 1 and 2 are the two halves of last week's CMOS inverter, each with a resistor standing in for the other transistor.

n-channel against p-channel · practice parts

The practice parts share VT = 1.5 V, with βn = 0.18 A/V² and βp = 0.08 A/V². At every gate drive, the p-channel resistance stays 2.25 times the n-channel's, which is the ratio of the two β values.

Same size and same drive: \(R_{DS(on),p} / R_{DS(on),n} = \beta_n/\beta_p\).

Common mistakes

  • Using the square law in part b. The switch is in triode, and the square law overstates its current almost eight times.
  • Dropping the ½ in the square law, which halves \(\beta\).
  • Leaving out the \(-\tfrac12 V_{DS}^2\) term in the triode formula. It's small at 0.2 V, but it moves the third significant figure.
  • Forgetting \(V_T\) and using \(V_{GS}\) alone as the overdrive.
  • Reading the p-channel switch's voltages from ground. \(V_{SG}\) and \(V_{SD}\) are measured from the source, which sits at 5 V.
  • Giving the p-channel drain current as positive. It flows out of the drain, so \(I_D\) is negative, and \(R_{DS(on)}\) uses \(|I_D|\).
  • Rounding \(\beta\) before using it again. Keep every digit, or keep it as a fraction.
